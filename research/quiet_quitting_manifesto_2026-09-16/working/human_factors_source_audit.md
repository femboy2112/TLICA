# Adversarial human-factors source audit: the “fuck-it factor”

**Manuscript audited:** Ethical Quiet Quitting Manifesto v1.4, especially §§6–6.5 and related claims elsewhere  
**Scope:** the acute-overload mechanism only  
**Audit date:** 16 August 2026  
**Epistemic standard:** a claim is treated as established only when the cited evidence establishes that claim at the stated level of generality. A useful analogy or plausible synthesis is not silently upgraded into a measured law.

## Bottom-line verdict

The section is publishable as a manifesto’s original synthesis, but it is not publication-ready if its equations are allowed to look like established human-factors laws.

The evidence supports this narrower core:

1. Time pressure, acute stress, interruptions, production pressure, inadequate resources, and conflicting goals can increase error risk, procedural departures, or both. Effects vary by task, expertise, outcome measure, and context.
2. Researchers distinguish unintentional errors from intentional violations. A violation may be an adaptive attempt to complete work; it may also introduce risk. Intentional departure does not imply intent to harm.
3. Workarounds are ambivalent. They can preserve service under a badly designed system, but they can also obscure recurring defects, create new failure paths, spread through a work group, and become routine.
4. Repeated organizational acceptance of anomalies or violations can normalize a departure. That literature does not establish the manuscript’s proposed within-person linear threshold recursion.
5. Near saturation, small losses of effective capacity can produce disproportionately large queues, delays, and coordination problems in appropriate system models. This supports the possibility of nonlinear consequences, not a universal workplace law.
6. Decisions made by people who control staffing, schedules, priorities, or reporting can propagate farther than a frontline worker’s local decision. The mechanism is scope of authority, not proof that managers experience the same internal threshold state.

The literature does **not** presently establish:

- a validated psychological construct called the fuck-it factor;
- a distinct, measurable threshold event that adds causal risk beyond the overload that selects people into it;
- the inequality $P(M_t\mid F_t=1)>P(M_t\mid F_t=0)$ as written;
- the recurrence $\theta_{t+1}\approx\theta_t-\eta F_t+\lambda Q_t$;
- a universal two-stage causal sequence;
- a fully validated closed feedback loop linking triage, mistakes, conflict, trust, slack, and later under-capacity;
- that every consequence following withdrawal is an “honest measurement” of pre-existing under-capacity.

Those are Leah’s hypotheses and conceptual synthesis. They can remain if unmistakably labeled as such.

## Naming verdict: keep it, with a construct-status disclaimer

Do **not** rename the fuck-it factor without Leah’s decision. The phrase carries the phenomenological force the model is trying to preserve: the felt transition from “try to honor every requirement” to “one requirement must now be relinquished.” It is usable in a manifesto.

The manuscript should, however, state on first definition:

> **“The fuck-it factor” is my coinage for a reported or felt transition under acute constraint conflict. It is not an established human-factors construct, a validated scale, a clinical diagnosis, or a legal excuse.**

It should also define the ethical boundary:

> The term does not make every requirement eligible for sacrifice. When no available action preserves the safety floor, stopping, slowing, escalating, or refusing an unsafe instruction may be the least harmful available action.

The tradeoff belongs to Leah, not to an editor silently sanitizing the phrase:

- **Reasons to keep it:** fidelity to lived experience; memorability; emotional honesty; a phrase workers may recognize immediately.
- **Reasons an audience may resist it:** profanity can narrow institutional, academic, corporate-training, school, library, or mainstream-media uptake; some readers may hear nihilism, permission, or intent rather than forced triage; search, advertising, or workplace filters may reduce discoverability.
- **Liability/safety interpretation:** the name itself does not establish liability one way or another. The material risk is misinterpretation as endorsement of sabotage, negligence, or knowingly unsafe work. The explicit non-excuse, safety-floor, communication, and safe-stop language substantially reduces that risk. A jurisdiction-specific legal review would require counsel; human-factors evidence cannot answer it.

A sober descriptive gloss may accompany the coined name—“acute constraint-collapse triage”—but it should be presented as an explanation, not a replacement.

## Equation-by-equation audit

### 1. The fully compliant action set

Current form:

$$
\mathcal A_t^{\mathrm{all}}
=
\{a\in\mathcal A_t:a\text{ satisfies every live constraint before its deadline}\},
\qquad
\mathcal A_t^{\mathrm{all}}=\varnothing.
$$

This is a useful formalization of incompatible demands, not a measurement result. Two distinctions are necessary.

First, **objective infeasibility** and **time-bounded perceived infeasibility** are different. A route may exist but be unavailable to the worker because necessary information, authority, equipment, coordination, or deliberation time is missing. Conversely, a worker may understandably fail to see a route that exists. The phenomenological factor depends on the recognized action space, while a later incident analysis may reconstruct a larger one.

A more exact manuscript sentence is:

> Under sufficient conflict among demand, staffing, equipment, authority, information, interruption, and deadlines, the set may actually be empty—or may appear empty to the worker within the time and information available.

If the formalism is kept, consider defining a known/reachable set:

$$
\mathcal A_{t,\mathrm{reachable}}^{\mathrm{all}}
=
\{a\in\mathcal A_t:a\text{ is known, authorized, and executable in time, and preserves every non-negotiable constraint}\}.
$$

Second, “some action must occur” is too broad in a safety-critical context. Pausing, slowing, safe shutdown, escalation, refusal, or evacuation can be actions. The system may make them costly, but the model should not erase them. If all operational actions breach the safety floor, the ethical prescription must be to preserve the floor rather than rank it against service speed.

### 2. The weighted-loss choice

Current form:

$$
a_t^*
\approx
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{a\in\mathcal A_t}
\sum_i \widehat w_{i,t}\ell_i(a).
$$

This is a compact normative or “as-if” model. The reviewed evidence does not show that workers perform this optimization or possess stable commensurable weights. In practice:

- losses may be incomparable;
- consequences may be unknown;
- multiple people may control different actions;
- formal priorities and enacted priorities may conflict;
- sanctions, customer hostility, moral commitments, and safety constraints do not reduce cleanly to one scale;
- under acute stress, a person may use a habit, cue, or local heuristic rather than an optimizer.

The hats already help. Add one sentence:

> This is a schematic reconstruction of forced prioritization, not a claim that the worker computes a numerical optimum or can foresee every consequence.

The plain-language sentence should become:

> **Fuck it. I cannot preserve everything. I will try to sacrifice the least harmful thing I am ethically permitted to sacrifice.**

That is slightly less raw than the existing wording but prevents “least important” from implying that safety, consent, legality, or another protected floor is just another adjustable weight. If Leah wishes to preserve the exact lived sentence, follow it immediately with the ethical-floor qualification.

### 3. The overload-risk arrow

Current form:

$$
P(M_t)\uparrow
\quad\text{as overload and urgency increase}.
$$

The direction has evidential support only as a **context-sensitive risk statement**. Szalma, Hancock, and Quinn’s meta-analysis of time pressure reported a small overall detrimental performance effect with strong moderation; in many perceptual and cognitive tasks, time pressure increased speed while reducing accuracy. Experimental and observational studies likewise show errors or degraded accuracy in particular tasks. Yet effects are not uniform, and “mental workload” itself is a measurement-sensitive construct.

Publication-safe form:

$$
\text{In many tasks, greater time pressure, interruption, or acute overload can raise error risk,}
$$

with the prose:

> The size and even the direction of an observed effect can depend on the task, expertise, measure, and context; this is a risk relation, not a universal monotonic law.

The manuscript’s list—attention fragments, working memory fills, communication compresses, checks are forgotten—is rhetorically effective but reads as a settled chain. Make it conditional: “Attention may fragment; working-memory demands may exceed available control; communication may compress; sequences may be interrupted; checks may be missed.”

### 4. The conditional probability inequality

Current form:

$$
P(M_t\mid F_t=1)>P(M_t\mid F_t=0).
$$

This is the section’s most important evidential defect.

It has three identification problems:

1. **Confounding by severity.** The manuscript defines $F=1$ as occurring under severe overload, time pressure, and reduced clarity. Those same conditions can raise $M$. A crude comparison of $F=1$ and $F=0$ therefore cannot tell whether $F$ adds risk or merely marks more severe conditions.
2. **Definitional overlap.** If $M$ includes any intentional requirement departure, while $F$ is defined by knowingly abandoning a requirement, then the inequality is partly true by construction. It does not demonstrate an increased probability of harm or unintentional error.
3. **Adaptive triage counterexample.** When full compliance is impossible, deliberate prioritization may reduce net harm relative to persisting with a doomed full-compliance attempt, following an unusable procedure, or sacrificing the constraint by accident. Workaround literature explicitly shows both enabling and hazardous functions.

The safest option is to remove the inequality and write:

> Human-factors evidence supports elevated baseline risk under time pressure, interruption, acute stress, competing goals, and inadequate resources. This manifesto goes further by proposing a distinct felt transition: a worker knowingly relaxes one live requirement because no fully compliant route appears reachable in time. That transition can introduce new failure modes, but the literature reviewed here does not isolate an additional causal risk attributable to the coined threshold itself. Deliberate triage can sometimes reduce total harm.

If the equation’s visual role is indispensable, mark it as an explicit research hypothesis:

$$
\mathbf H_1:
P(Y_t^{\mathrm{harm}}\mid F_t=1,X_t)
>
P(Y_t^{\mathrm{harm}}\mid F_t=0,X_t),
$$

where $Y_t^{\mathrm{harm}}$ excludes the mere fact of a deliberate departure and $X_t$ includes objective demand, time pressure, task, expertise, staffing, interruptions, equipment state, authority, and prior fatigue. Even then, this is only a hypothesis; conditioning cannot by itself identify causation, and the counter-hypothesis that triage lowers harm remains live.

### 5. The threshold recurrence

Current form:

$$
\theta_{t+1}
\approx
\theta_t-\eta F_t+\lambda Q_t.
$$

The disclaimer “not a psychometric law” is necessary but insufficient, because the directional recurrence still visually asserts:

- one latent scalar threshold;
- a lowering effect after every activation;
- a constant positive effect of repair;
- linear and additive changes;
- no moderation by outcome, feedback, sanction, learning, values, role, team norms, or the content of the violated requirement.

The cited literature can support a weaker organizational claim. Routine or condoned departures may become normalized; workarounds can become familiar and embedded in work-group practice; successful first-order problem solving can conceal defects from people who could repair them. It does not establish this recurrence.

Best correction: delete the equation and rename the subsection **“Repetition can normalize the workaround.”**

Exact replacement:

> One episode may be emergency triage. Repeated, unexamined departures can become easier to retrieve, diffuse through a team, be built into local routines, or be treated as evidence that the formal process is unnecessary. That trajectory is not automatic. Consequences, feedback, supervision, debriefing, repair, sanctions, and work-group norms can interrupt or reverse it. The evidence supports the risk of routinization and normalization; it does not establish a universal within-person threshold that falls after every episode.

If Leah wants to preserve the equation as part of the manifesto’s conceptual signature, label it immediately before and after:

> The following is an illustrative hypothesis, not an empirically estimated recurrence.

and change the equality-like form to a noncommittal dependency:

$$
\theta_{t+1}
=
f(\theta_t,F_t,\text{outcome feedback},\text{repair},\text{recovery},
\text{norms},\text{sanctions},\text{task}),
$$

with no assumed sign for $F_t$ in every case.

### 6. The proposed feedback loop

Current loop:

$$
\text{under-capacity}
\rightarrow
\text{acute triage}
\rightarrow
\text{mistakes and conflict}
\rightarrow
\text{less trust and less slack}
\rightarrow
\text{greater under-capacity}.
$$

There is useful supporting evidence for several edges:

- operational failures and missing resources generate interruptions and workaround activity;
- high demands and burnout are associated with safety behavior and outcomes;
- local problem solving can keep work moving while preventing organizational learning;
- supervisory priority-setting shapes subunit safety climate and practice.

The complete loop has not been validated as a single causal model. “Conflict → less trust” is especially unsourced in this mechanism audit; it may be true in some settings, but it cannot be presented as empirically settled here.

Safer wording:

> These processes can form a reinforcing pathway. For example, inadequate capacity or unreliable tools can provoke triage and workaround use; unresolved workarounds can hide recurring causes, add cleanup and interruption, and leave less recoverable capacity for the next demand surge. The full loop below is this manifesto’s synthesis, not a validated universal model.

That loop is more closely tied to the workaround and organizational-learning evidence:

$$
\text{unrepaired work-system constraints}
\rightarrow
\text{local triage/workarounds}
\rightarrow
\text{hidden causes plus cleanup/interruptions}
\rightarrow
\text{less recoverable capacity}
\rightarrow
\text{greater exposure to the same constraints}.
$$

Trust may remain in the surrounding moral argument, but it should not appear as a measured mediator unless a specific source is added.

### 7. Capacity, slack, and nonlinearity

Current form:

$$
K_t=K_t^{\mathrm{base}}+H_t,\qquad
S_t=K_t-W_t.
$$

The equations work as a conceptual budget only if $K$, $H$, and $W$ are defined in compatible units over the same time window. Real service systems also contain:

- bottleneck-specific rather than fully fungible capacity;
- different skill and authorization classes;
- arrival variability;
- setup and handoff costs;
- equipment constraints;
- priority classes;
- quality and safety floors;
- delayed work and rework.

Therefore $S<0$ does not by itself prove that the fully compliant action set is empty, and $S>0$ does not guarantee feasibility. One scarce authorized person or one failed tool can empty the relevant action set despite aggregate capacity; conversely, reprioritization may preserve non-negotiable constraints despite demand exceeding a nominal target.

The nonlinear intuition is nonetheless defensible when stated as a possibility. Queueing results show that waiting can grow sharply as utilization approaches one in simple service models, and resilience theory describes systems that appear stable until adaptive capacity is exhausted. These are disciplined analogies, not direct validation of the manifesto’s variables.

Replace:

> “It is the first honest measurement of under-capacity.”

with:

> A downstream disruption can reveal that the prior operating point depended on hidden adaptive capacity. It is evidence of that dependence, but not by itself proof that under-capacity is the only cause; sequencing, demand shocks, bottlenecks, skill mix, coordination, or the way the withdrawal was implemented may also matter.

This preserves the manifesto’s important moral point—exploitation is not vindicated merely because withdrawing unpaid heroics has effects—without turning an observed consequence into a clean causal identification.

### 8. Managerial scaling and power

The strongest empirical footing is not “managers have the same factor,” because the internal phenomenology is unmeasured. The footing is that people with control over common conditions can expose many workers to the same priority structure. Zohar’s field intervention shows that supervisory monitoring and reinforcement of safety priorities can alter subunit safety climate and accompany changes in safety behavior and injury indicators. Safety-climate research more broadly supports the importance of enacted priority when safety competes with speed or schedules.

Safer passage:

> Managers and executives are subject to acute stress and constrained judgment too, but the phrase “fuck-it factor” names an internal transition and should not be diagnosed from outside. What can be observed is a decision such as “just get it done,” suppression of a warning, or a labor cut that passes incompatible constraints downward. When the decision-maker controls staffing, scheduling, priorities, or reporting, one decision can alter the action space of everyone under that authority.

Then retain:

> **Humanness is symmetric. Power is not.**

as a normative thesis. Follow it with:

> The scale comes from control of shared constraints, not from title alone; a frontline manager may be trapped by constraints imposed above, while a worker in a safety-critical role may sometimes hold significant stop-work power.

That last sentence prevents the hierarchy claim from becoming a crude manager/worker binary.

## Terminology correction: error is not violation

The manuscript currently defines $M_t$ as a “materially mistaken outcome” and then includes an “intentional departure” as a mistake. Human-factors taxonomies generally distinguish:

- **error:** an unintended failure of plan, memory, perception, judgment, or execution;
- **violation:** an intentional departure from a rule or procedure, without implying intent to cause harm;
- **adverse outcome:** harm, loss, delay, or degraded quality that may arise from error, violation, system conditions, or a mixed cascade.

Reason and colleagues’ foundational work treats errors and violations as psychologically distinct, and NASA’s current handbook likewise separates human error and intentional noncompliance/violation categories. Alper and Karsh’s systematic review defines safety violations as intentional, non-malevolent deviations and identifies multiple classes of predictors, including competing goals and rule problems.

Exact revision:

> Let $D_t$ denote a materially significant departure or adverse outcome, and let $E_t$ denote an unintentional error. The possible pathways include intentional procedural departures, unintentional errors, and mixed cascades in which a deliberate shortcut produces an unintended effect. “Intentional” describes awareness of the departure, not desire for harm.

If $M_t$ must stay, define it strictly as unintended error or harm, not as the departure itself. Otherwise the conditional probability claim is circular.

## What each evidence family actually establishes

### Workload and time pressure

The 2008 meta-analysis by Szalma, Hancock, and Quinn synthesized 125 references and hundreds of effects. It supports a small overall detrimental effect of time pressure with substantial task and measure moderation; speed can increase while accuracy falls. It does not establish that every increase in urgency causes more mistakes or that a single latent overload variable has a universal monotonic effect.

Tsiga and colleagues’ within-subject experiment with general practitioners found that time pressure changed consultation behavior and reduced some guideline-concordant information gathering and advice. Sussman and Sekuler’s laboratory experiment found that induced time pressure reduced accuracy and cognitive inhibition in a flanker task and increased stress/negative affect. These are useful primary demonstrations, but neither directly observes the fuck-it factor.

Young and colleagues’ state-of-science review warns that mental workload remains conceptually and operationally difficult. This supports using specific exposures—arrival rate, concurrent tasks, interruptions, time-to-deadline, staffing, equipment availability—instead of treating “overload” as a self-explanatory scalar.

### Acute stress and executive control

Shields, Sazma, and Yonelinas’ meta-analysis supports differentiated effects: acute stress impaired working memory, cognitive flexibility, and cognitive inhibition on average, while response inhibition could improve. The manuscript may say reduced clarity is plausible under acute stress; it should not say all executive control uniformly collapses.

This literature supports uncertainty in the estimated weights $\widehat w_{i,t}$. It does not establish the manuscript’s optimizer, the existence of a discrete threshold, or the incremental conditional probability inequality.

### Interruptions

Westbrook and colleagues observed associations between interruptions and medication-administration errors, with risk increasing with the number of interruptions. In emergency prescribing, interrupted tasks and multitasking were associated with higher prescribing error risk; notably, one workload measure in that model was not independently associated with error. Another observational study found that clinicians sometimes failed to return to interrupted tasks.

These studies support interruption as a concrete risk mechanism in clinical work. They remain observational and domain-specific. The publication-safe claim is “interruptions can disrupt sequences and are associated with error in several safety-critical settings,” not “every interruption causes an error.”

### Production pressure, competing goals, and violations

Hashemian and Triantis’ systematic review links production pressure with safety-relevant behavior and outcomes across sociotechnical systems, while emphasizing the need to define and manage the pressure. Alper and Karsh’s systematic review found a multi-causal violation literature, with competing goals and problems with rules among the predictor categories. Lawton and Hale/Borys explain why rigid procedures and situated work can diverge.

This evidence supports the proposition that “work faster / produce more” can conflict with safety or procedure and that violations may be shaped by the system. It does not excuse a specific unsafe act, prove that pressure forced the act, or establish that every procedural departure was the least harmful option.

### Workarounds and adaptive triage

Debono and colleagues’ scoping review is essential counterevidence to a one-directional “threshold crossed → more mistakes” model. Workarounds can enable care or task completion when systems do not fit the work, while also potentially compromising safety. Many studies did not directly measure outcomes. Koppel and colleagues documented numerous medication-system workarounds and their design/implementation causes, including new error pathways.

Tucker, Edmondson, and Spear show why successful local problem solving can be organizationally dangerous without being locally irrational: it restores the immediate process but can keep managers from seeing and repairing recurring causes.

Therefore the manifesto should say:

> Deliberate triage is neither inherently safe nor inherently mistaken. It may be the least harmful local response, and it may still create hidden risk or prevent system learning.

### Repetition and normalization of deviance

NASA’s 2023 Human Factors Handbook classifies routine/widespread workaround violations and explains how habitual noncompliance that is condoned or not disciplined may constitute normalization of deviance. The Columbia Accident Investigation Board report is an official case source for organizational acceptance of recurring anomalies and the erosion of safety margins.

Rasmussen’s dynamic-society model is a theoretical account of organizations migrating toward performance boundaries under cost, workload, and effort gradients. It is highly relevant conceptually, but it is not a measured law of individual threshold movement.

Liang and colleagues found cross-sectional associations between coworkers’ violations, perceived norms/production pressure, and individuals’ violation behavior in construction. That supports possible social diffusion, but the design cannot prove that one episode causes a lower within-person threshold.

Thus:

- **Supported:** repeated or condoned departures can become routine or normative at a group/organizational level.
- **Not established:** every activation lowers the same individual’s scalar threshold by $\eta$.
- **Live counterpossibility:** a bad outcome, debrief, sanction, rest, training, or moral injury might raise avoidance; repetition may also improve safe triage skill rather than lower standards.

### Decision fatigue

Do not use “decision fatigue” as a foundation for the section. A 2025 preregistered systematic review of 82 healthcare studies found significant support in only 45% of quantitative cases and emphasized inconsistent definitions and inadequate operationalization. A 2025 large registered field study of more than 231,000 calls handled by 174 nurses found evidence favoring the null for its main tests and questioned a domain-general decision-fatigue effect.

This does not show that long shifts, fatigue, or cumulative demands are harmless. It shows that the attractive label “decision fatigue” is too unsettled to do causal work here. The manuscript already has stronger and more specific constructs: acute stress, working-memory demand, time pressure, interruption, sleep/fatigue where separately sourced, and conflicting goals.

### Organizational scaling and power

Zohar’s intervention supports a causal role for supervisory practice in the safety priority communicated at the subunit level. Nahrgang, Morgeson, and Hofmann’s meta-analysis across 203 independent samples links job demands and resources with burnout, engagement, safety behavior, and safety outcomes. Together these support the claim that work design and supervisory priorities affect more than one isolated decision.

They do not establish:

- identical internal phenomenology at every hierarchical level;
- that every manager has more practical power than every worker;
- that one manager’s bad judgment necessarily becomes policy.

Use “can propagate,” and anchor propagation to the number of people whose constraints the decision controls.

### Nonlinear consequences

Kingman’s heavy-traffic result is a canonical demonstration that queueing behavior can become sharply worse near saturation in a single-server model. Woods’s theory of graceful extensibility describes how finite adaptive capacity and saturation can produce brittleness or abrupt performance loss. These sources support the mechanism’s possibility.

They do not prove the specific additive capacity equation, identify a workplace’s threshold, or show that every withdrawal of hidden effort causes a discontinuity. The manuscript should explicitly say that the nonlinearity is a model-based warning and that empirical application requires measuring arrival variability, service capacity, bottlenecks, priorities, and recovery.

## Proposed publication-ready replacement for the core of §6

The following text preserves the phenomenological phrase while keeping evidence and synthesis separate:

> **The fuck-it factor** is my term for an acute felt transition: a person moves from trying to satisfy the whole live constraint set to recognizing, under time pressure and reduced clarity, that no fully compliant action appears reachable before action is required. It is a phenomenological coinage, not an established human-factors construct, validated scale, diagnosis, or legal excuse.
>
> The action set may actually be empty, or it may appear empty within the time, information, equipment, and authority available to the person. The distinction matters to later analysis, but both can shape the decision in the moment. Stopping, slowing, escalating, refusing an unsafe instruction, or entering a safe shutdown are also actions; when no operational action preserves the safety floor, the safety floor is not merely another weight to trade away.
>
> Human-factors research supports the surrounding risk conditions. Time pressure often produces a speed–accuracy tradeoff; acute stress can impair working memory and cognitive flexibility; interruptions can disrupt sequences; production pressure and conflicting goals can contribute to procedural departures. These effects vary by task, expertise, and setting. They establish elevated contextual risk, not a universal law and not the coined threshold itself.
>
> Under those conditions, a person may knowingly relax one ethically eligible requirement in an attempt to preserve more important ones. Researchers distinguish that intentional departure from an unintentional error, although either can contribute to an adverse outcome and a deliberate shortcut can have an unintended consequence. Deliberate triage may reduce total harm; it may also introduce a failure path the person cannot see.
>
> The further proposal of this manifesto is that workers sometimes experience a recognizable transition into that deliberate triage state: **Fuck it. I cannot preserve everything. I will try to sacrifice the least harmful thing I am permitted to sacrifice.** Whether that transition adds risk beyond the severe conditions that trigger it is an empirical question, not something the phrase itself proves.

Replacement for §6.2:

> **Repetition can normalize the workaround.** One episode may be emergency triage. Repeated, unexamined departures can become easier to retrieve, spread through a team, be incorporated into local routines, hide recurring system defects, or be treated as evidence that the original constraint was unnecessary. This is not automatic. Feedback, consequences, supervision, debriefing, rest, repair, and work-group norms can interrupt or reverse the trajectory. The literature supports a risk of routinization and organizational normalization; it does not establish that every episode lowers a person’s threshold.

Replacement qualification for §6.3:

> Quiet quitting and acute triage have a family resemblance: each can arise when a valued pathway appears closed at its own time scale. Calling them “duals” is this manifesto’s conceptual synthesis, not an established finding. They may interact, but the direction and strength of that interaction remain to be tested.

Replacement for the key inference in §6.4:

> When a system was operating near a bottleneck, withdrawing hidden heroic capacity can have effects larger than the apparent reduction in labor. Queueing and resilience models make that possibility intelligible, but they do not predict every workplace. A disruption after withdrawal can reveal dependence on hidden adaptive capacity without proving that under-capacity was the only cause.

Replacement qualification for §6.5:

> The phrase names an internal transition and should not be diagnosed from outside. What is observable is the decision and its reach. A worker’s local departure may affect one task; a decision by someone who controls staffing, priorities, scheduling, equipment, or reporting can alter the action space of a whole unit. **Humanness is symmetric. Power is not.** The asymmetry lies in control of shared constraints, not in a claim that every person at one level has the same psychology or the same power.

## A falsifiable research path for Leah’s novel synthesis

If the manifesto later becomes a paper, the new construct needs to earn its separation from ordinary overload, intentional violation, and workaround behavior.

### Construct definition

Pre-register an event as $F=1$ only when all are present:

1. the person was initially trying to preserve multiple live requirements;
2. before a deadline, the person judged that no known, authorized, executable action could preserve them all;
3. the person knowingly selected at least one ethically eligible requirement to relax;
4. the person reports a recognizable transition into deliberate triage;
5. the event is not merely an accidental slip, forgetting, generalized disengagement, routine noncompliance, or malicious intent.

Measure objective constraints separately from the person’s perceived/reachable action set.

### Discriminating probes

1. **Incremental-risk probe:** At matched levels of time pressure, interruption, staffing, task complexity, expertise, and fatigue, does reported $F$ predict unintended error or harm? If not, $F$ may be a phenomenological marker rather than an added risk mechanism.
2. **Adaptive-triage probe:** In objectively impossible scenarios, does $F$ reduce harm relative to persistence, arbitrary choice, or unrecognized failure? A positive result would refute the manuscript’s unqualified conditional inequality while supporting the construct’s ethical importance.
3. **Threshold-repetition probe:** In repeated simulations, does one $F$ event change the pressure level at which the next transition occurs? Randomize feedback, repair, rest, sanction, and outcome. The sign may vary.
4. **Distinctness probe:** Do workers distinguish $F$ from ordinary frustration, burnout, fatigue, rule-bending, “getting by,” and safe-stop judgment? If ratings collapse onto existing constructs, the new name may remain valuable phenomenologically without warranting a separate scientific construct.
5. **Power/propagation probe:** Model reach using the number of downstream workers and constraints controlled by a decision. Test whether propagation is explained by authority scope rather than role label or inferred internal state.

### Evidence that would weaken the model

The manifesto should be prepared for these outcomes:

- workers report no discrete transition, only continuous prioritization;
- deliberate triage lowers net harm at every matched severity level;
- repetition does not lower later thresholds, or raises them after bad outcomes;
- objective infeasibility is rare once safe-stop and escalation are included;
- “fuck-it factor” responses are not separable from established violation/workaround constructs;
- manager propagation is explained entirely by formal authority, without a distinct acute psychology.

None of these would erase the phrase’s phenomenological value. They would change what kind of claim it can bear.

## Priority edits before publication

1. Keep the name, add the coinage/non-validated/non-excuse disclaimer on first use.
2. Replace “least important” with “least harmful ethically eligible,” or immediately add a safety-floor qualification.
3. Distinguish objective infeasibility from perceived/reachable infeasibility; include stop, slow, escalate, refuse, and safe shutdown in the action set.
4. Change universal overload language to conditional risk language.
5. Remove $P(M_t\mid F_t=1)>P(M_t\mid F_t=0)$ or label it a testable hypothesis with non-circular outcomes and confounders.
6. Stop calling intentional departures “mistakes”; distinguish errors, violations, adverse outcomes, and mixed cascades.
7. Replace the threshold recurrence with prose about possible routinization/normalization, or mark it explicitly as an illustrative hypothesis with no universal sign.
8. Label the short/long “dual” and the full feedback loop as Leah’s conceptual synthesis.
9. Qualify nonlinearity as model-based possibility; replace “first honest measurement” with a weaker causal inference.
10. Anchor managerial scaling to control of shared constraints; do not diagnose an internal manager threshold from an order alone.
11. Do not rely on “decision fatigue.” Use specific, better-supported exposures.
12. Add a short methods/provenance note separating (a) established human-factors findings, (b) theoretical analogies, and (c) Leah’s original phenomenological synthesis.

## Final assessment

The “fuck-it factor” is strongest when it does not impersonate a laboratory variable. Its publishable contribution is the act of naming a morally and phenomenologically important transition that adjacent literatures describe only in pieces: impossible or apparently impossible constraints, competing goals, local adaptation, intentional departure, error risk, workaround normalization, and unequal authority over the conditions of work.

The evidence can protect that contribution if the manuscript says exactly where the science ends:

> The surrounding risk ecology is established. The coined threshold, its incremental causal effect, its recurrence, and its long/short-horizon duality are Leah’s proposed synthesis.

That sentence would make the section more credible without sacrificing its force.
