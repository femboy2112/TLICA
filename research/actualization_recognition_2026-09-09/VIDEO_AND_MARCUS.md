## 3. Why an impossible scene can feel more faithful than a sensible explanation

The essay's characterization contrast is useful because it places pressure on the relations preserved by a representation. A character's optimism can influence several possible actions without selecting every answer in advance. A listener who recognizes the label but not its context-dependent consequences may produce a sensible paraphrase that nevertheless changes the object.

Our conceptual distinction is **interactional fidelity versus literal realism**. A fictional worm, monster, or impossible event can make a recognizable manner of responding available. A realistic setting can instead give everyone the same functional dialogue. Neither medium determines the outcome. The test is whether the relevant particulars still have consequences.

This does not imply a duty to preserve every detail. The object may permit compression. Fidelity requires knowing which details carry the distinction at issue; it is not maximal description or a refusal to edit.

## 4. Sincerity without literalness

The essay complicates the notion that authenticity means removing performance. A deliberate character may express something that ordinary presentation does not. Humor can carry affection, criticism, or vulnerability without cancelling them. Conversely, a apparently candid presentation can still mislead.

Four questions remain separate: is the expression owned; is it faithful to its declared object; are its factual implications supported; is the conduct ethically defensible? No one of these answers supplies the others.

'Commit to the bit' also has two possible meanings. It can mean giving a chosen activity enough continuity to become something. Or it can mean continuing regardless of correction and cost. The first resembles correctable commitment; the second can preserve a performance by sacrificing the person or purpose it was supposed to serve.

## 8. Permission is not enough, and recognition can become a demand

A relationship that appreciates an unusual mode of arrival can still misunderstand the person. It can also start demanding that the person keep arriving in that mode. 'Do the voice' and 'be the strange one' can become affectionate commands to reproduce a role.

The useful question is whether change remains admissible. Can the person be unfunny, ordinary, unavailable, or serious without losing their standing? Can the scene partner respond to a departure from the bit instead of requiring its continuation? These are proposed questions; no answer about the actual performers has been established here.

The deepest connection to the author's recognition problem is therefore not that unexplained eccentricity should always be celebrated. It is that the particulars must be allowed to influence what the encounter becomes. A preset can erase those particulars through ridicule, sympathy, admiration, or professional analysis alike.
