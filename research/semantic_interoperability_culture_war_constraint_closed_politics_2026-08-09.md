## 0. Executive synthesis

The discussion motivating this dossier converged on one object:

> **Democratic politics is not only a contest among propositions or policy preferences. It is an emergent contest among socially learned representations of reality, carried by candidates and parties that resonate with enough differently situated voters to form governing coalitions.**

Every person encounters a partly shared world through a private history of place, class, religion, race, occupation, education, media, family, trauma, status, culture, and remembered analogy. Those histories produce nonidentical representational systems. The same event, word, candidate, or institution can therefore evoke different causal stories, moral weights, identity implications, and threats in different people.

This makes the ordinary political word **vibe** analytically useful rather than dismissible. A candidate's vibe is the voter's low-dimensional, affectively weighted model of the governing world that candidate appears to represent. The model can be coherent with the voter while being false about the world. Resonance is not truth.

Culture war is a limiting case in which:

1. within-group semantic bandwidth becomes high;
2. cross-group semantic interoperability becomes low;
3. political representations become strongly identity-coupled;
4. the same public vocabulary invokes materially different internal objects;
5. political entrepreneurs profit from loading entire in-group and out-group models through compressed tokens, slogans, scenes, and memes.

The proposed repair is not generic civility and not arithmetic midpoint bargaining. It is **constraint-closed compromise**:

> A political viewpoint must accept the foreseeable downstream commitments generated by its own principles, and it must permanently incorporate the strongest consequential variable emphasized by a rival viewpoint that survives factual, causal, rights, and source-map review.

The intuitive target is a **cohesive total viewpoint**. “Total” must remain bounded: no person or institution can track literally every consequence. The operational object is therefore a **bounded constraint-closed viewpoint**—a representation that, for a declared decision boundary, tracks all load-bearing verified variables and downstream obligations revealed by adversarial review.

The key distinction is:

\[
\boxed{\text{real compromise} \neq \text{splitting the distance between slogans}}
\]

Real compromise seeks a policy inside a jointly expanded constraint surface. It may produce a midpoint, one side's original proposal, no permissible compromise, or a third solution neither side initially represented.

## 1. Recovering the object

### 1.1 What is being modeled

The object is not “why voters are irrational.” It is the representation-and-aggregation chain through which a large population produces elected government:

\[
\text{lived environment}
\rightarrow
\text{learned representational basis}
\rightarrow
\text{salience, identity, and value}
\rightarrow
\text{candidate resonance}
\rightarrow
\text{electoral aggregation}
\rightarrow
\text{governing institution}.
\]

Every arrow has feedback. Governments change laws, schools, media incentives, economic conditions, geography, status distributions, and available public concepts. Political representations are therefore both products and causes of the environments that sustain them.

### 1.2 What “elite-coded” and “folk-coded” are trying to capture

The author's initial shorthand contrasted “high-class vibes” and “low-class vibes.” This should not be preserved as a literal historical class ranking. The stronger object is a contrast among **status-coded and resolution-coded political styles**.

An elite-coded representation often privileges:

- abstraction;
- institutions and procedures;
- expert categories;
- statistics and long causal chains;
- formal education and credentialed authority;
- cosmopolitan or nationally standardized language.

A folk- or populist-coded representation often privileges:

- concrete agents and directly recognizable harms;
- local experience and testimony;
- heroes, villains, loyalty, humiliation, and betrayal;
- culturally familiar symbols;
- intuitive causal narratives;
- authenticity and suspicion of distant abstraction.

Neither basis is intrinsically true or false. Each can throw away consequential information:

\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{elite compression loss:} & \text{lived detail, local knowledge, embodiment, unequal burden};\\
\text{folk compression loss:} & \text{structural causes, base rates, institutional constraints, long-term externalities}.
\end{array}
\]

The political task is not to declare one basis superior in every regime. It is to identify which variables each basis tracks and discards.

## 2. A formal sketch of private political representation

Let \(\mathcal W_t\) denote the relevant world-state at time \(t\). Person \(i\) does not receive \(\mathcal W_t\) directly. Their history \(h_i\), environment \(e_i\), social position \(s_i\), and conceptual toolkit \(B_i\) contribute to a learned map:

\[
L_i : (\mathcal W_t,h_i,e_i,s_i,B_i) \rightarrow r_i(t),
\]

where \(r_i(t)\) is the person's current internal representation of the relevant political world.

A candidate \(c\) emits messages, performances, policies, symbols, affiliations, vocal styles, and biographical cues collected schematically as \(m_c\). Person \(i\) decodes them through their own basis:

\[
\widehat M_{ic}=D_i(m_c;B_i,h_i,s_i).
\]

Define a schematic **resonance functional**:

\[
R_i(c)
=
\operatorname{Coh}
\bigl(
\widehat M_{ic},
 r_i(t),
 v_i,
 \rho_i,
 a_i
\bigr),
\]

where:

- \(v_i\) denotes value commitments;
- \(\rho_i\) denotes identity or commitment coupling, not truth;
- \(a_i\) denotes current affective and material state;
- \(\operatorname{Coh}\) measures experienced fit, not correspondence to reality.

A candidate's “vibe” is the integrated, low-dimensional output of this decoding process. The voter may not be able to articulate every coordinate, yet still perceive the candidate as:

- one of us;
- competent;
- fake;
- strong;
- compassionate;
- humiliating;
- elite;
- dangerous;
- fair;
- corrupt;
- able to restore order.

The probability of support can be written only schematically as:

\[
\Pr_i(c)
=
f(R_i(c),p_i,q_i,n_i,\ldots),
\]

where \(p_i\) includes policy preferences, \(q_i\) performance judgments, and \(n_i\) institutional or social pressures.

The model does **not** claim that resonance fully determines voting. It states that resonance is a real compression layer through which detailed political information is often made usable.

Research on low-information rationality, political symbols, candidate inference, and policy-following supplies adjacent prior art. See Samuel Popkin's *The Reasoning Voter*, Gabriel Lenz's *Follow the Leader?*, Campbell et al.'s *The American Voter*, and Kinder and Kalmoe's *Neither Liberal nor Conservative* in the references.

### 2.1 Electoral aggregation

Let an electoral rule \(\mathcal E\) aggregate the decisions of \(N\) voters:

\[
\mathcal E
\bigl(
\Pr_1(c),\Pr_2(c),\ldots,\Pr_N(c)
\bigr)
\rightarrow
\text{office-holder}.
\]

One person then becomes a highly compressed political representative of millions of nonidentical internal systems.

A successful coalition candidate is therefore a **semantic multiplexing device**. A token such as “freedom,” “family,” “order,” “change,” or “justice” can be underdetermined enough that several constituencies decode it favorably for different reasons.

This is not automatically deception. Political language must coordinate diverse groups. But the same ambiguity can be exploited to conceal incompatible commitments until after power is secured.

## 3. Semantic interoperability and its failure

The companion note defines communication as transport between private representational systems:

\[
S
\xrightarrow{E_A}
 m
\xrightarrow{D_B}
\widehat S_B.
\]

Communication succeeds relative to declared invariants \(I\) when:

\[
\widehat S_B\sim_I S.
\]

Define **semantic interoperability** informally as the probability that differently situated people can reconstruct the load-bearing structure of one another's representations with tolerable loss.

Interoperability is independent of agreement:

\[
\boxed{\text{understood} \neq \text{accepted} \neq \text{true}.}
\]

A person can accurately reconstruct an opponent's model and reject it. Two people can share a fluent false model. A common vocabulary can conceal divergent internal relations.

### 3.1 Memes, canons, and political compression

Shared cultural objects function as semantic addresses. “Sisyphean” can invoke futile recurring labor. “Weekend at Bernie's” can invoke an institution whose body persists after its animating purpose has died. Political memes work similarly.

Inside a community:

\[
\text{shared prior structure}\uparrow
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\text{explicit transmission cost}\downarrow.
\]

But basis-specific compression can reduce cross-group fidelity. Terms such as “woke,” “fascist,” “DEI,” “groomer,” “globalist,” “socialist,” or “religious freedom” may load an entire in-group model while conveying a caricature to outsiders.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{within-group semantic bandwidth}\uparrow
\quad\text{can coexist with}\quad
\text{cross-group interoperability}\downarrow.
}
\]

## 4. Culture war as codec divergence

A culture-war object \(x\) is decoded through a culturally learned basis:

\[
x
\xrightarrow{B_i}
\bigl(
\text{meaning},
\text{cause},
\text{moral weight},
\text{identity relevance},
\text{threat}
\bigr)_i.
\]

Different groups can therefore use the same lexical token while receiving different objects.

Culture war becomes especially intense when four conditions combine:

1. **basis divergence** — the groups possess different histories, media, exemplars, and causal schemas;
2. **identity sorting** — partisan identity aligns with religion, race, region, education, status, or culture;
3. **moralization** — political disagreement becomes evidence of moral defect;
4. **recursive modeling** — each side reacts to its model of the other side's model of itself.

Research on affective polarization and social sorting supports adjacent parts of this picture. Iyengar et al. review the growth of out-party dislike and distrust; Mason and Kalmoe emphasize alignment of partisan identity with race, religion, region, and culture; Garrett and Bankert show that moral conviction can intensify partisan hostility.

The proposed semantic model adds a distinct diagnostic layer:

\[
\boxed{
\text{culture war}
\approx
\text{identity-coupled conflict among increasingly noninteroperable representations}.
}
\]

This is not a complete reduction. Some conflicts remain after perfect understanding because interests, values, rights, or material outcomes are genuinely incompatible.

### 4.1 Five different obstructions

A serious political diagnosis should separate at least five possibilities:

| Obstruction | Question |
|---|---|
| **Codec gap** | Are the parties reconstructing one another's intended claims? |
| **Source-map gap** | Do they rely on different evidence, authorities, or causal histories? |
| **Empirical gap** | Which claims survive measurement, primary sources, and causal testing? |
| **Value gap** | Do they understand the facts but rank persons, rights, or outcomes differently? |
| **Power/strategy gap** | Is one side bargaining in bad faith, protecting advantage, or using dialogue instrumentally? |

Only the first is solved by translation alone.

## 5. Parties as persistent labels over changing coalitions

A political party should not be modeled as one timeless ideology. Represent party \(j\) at time \(t\) as:

\[
P_j(t)
=
\bigl(
\ell_j,
K_j(t),
B_j(t),
\Pi_j(t)
\bigr),
\]

where:

- \(\ell_j\) is the persistent party label;
- \(K_j(t)\) is the coalition of constituencies;
- \(B_j(t)\) is the party's operative semantic and ideological basis;
- \(\Pi_j(t)\) is its policy and institutional program.

Then:

\[
\ell_j(t_1)=\ell_j(t_2)
\]

can remain true while:

\[
K_j(t_1)\neq K_j(t_2),
\qquad
B_j(t_1)\neq B_j(t_2),
\qquad
\Pi_j(t_1)\neq \Pi_j(t_2).
\]

A historical realignment is not necessarily a binary “party switch.” It is the migration and transformation of multiple basis vectors at different rates.

### 5.1 A bounded American sketch

The following is a schematic historical orientation, not a complete party history.

- During Reconstruction, the national Republican coalition was associated with Union victory, emancipation's political consequences, federal enforcement, and Black political participation. White Southern Democrats became the main political vehicle for restoring white local rule and protecting the post-Confederate racial order. The South was not merely “low class”: planter elites held enormous power.
- During the New Deal, many Black voters moved toward the Democratic Party in response to material relief and opportunities in northern urban coalitions, while powerful Southern segregationist Democrats remained inside the same national party. The House historical archive documents this internally contradictory coalition and the gradual Black partisan shift.
- In the civil-rights era, the national Democratic Party increasingly supported federal civil-rights enforcement while Southern Democrats led much of the opposition. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 passed through a bipartisan coalition; Southern opposition was decisive, and Republican Senate support was indispensable. Barry Goldwater opposed the Act and carried five Deep South states, but Southern white realignment remained gradual rather than instantaneous.

The proper historical question is therefore not:

> Were nineteenth-century Republicans “the same” as today's Republicans?

It is:

> Which regional, racial, religious, economic, constitutional, and status-coded basis vectors occupied each coalition at each time, how did those vectors change, and where did they migrate?

Primary and official orientation sources include the House history of Black partisan realignment and the National Archives' Civil Rights Act records.

### 5.2 Goldwater as semantic-basis drift under a persistent identifier

Barry Goldwater is analytically useful because he helped construct modern conservative Republican politics and later objected to a constituency increasingly acting under the same label.

In a September 15, 1981 Senate speech, Goldwater argued that religious factions were pressuring officials to follow their positions completely, treating disagreement as moral failure, and trying to dictate moral convictions “in the name of conservatism.” The official Congressional Record for that day is preserved by Congress.gov; contemporary reporting recorded his claim that the “New Right” was not his conservatism and his warning that religious factions could damage representative government.

This is not evidence that Goldwater's own politics were internally pure or that his 1964 role should be softened. It is evidence for a narrower proposition:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the same token “conservative” can persist while its operative coalition and semantic basis drift.}
}
\]

Goldwater saw a conservative grammar centered on limited government and private liberty being joined or displaced by a grammar in which some moral truths were treated as requiring public enforcement and compromise could itself appear immoral.

That is a historical instance of semantic continuity at the lexical level masking semantic transformation at the structural level.

## 6. Why ordinary political compromise fails

Political compromise is often represented as arithmetic moderation:

\[
\pi_{\mathrm{mid}}
=
\lambda \pi_A+(1-\lambda)\pi_B.
\]

But slogans are not sufficient representations of the underlying problem. Splitting the distance between two incomplete models can preserve the omissions of both.

The relevant task is:

1. recover each side's principles and factual map;
2. expose the variables each representation throws away;
3. test those variables against evidence and rights boundaries;
4. close each viewpoint under its own foreseeable commitments;
5. search the resulting joint constraint surface.

### 6.1 Bounded constraint closure

Let a viewpoint be:

\[
W=(P,F,V,\Pi),
\]

where:

- \(P\) = declared principles;
- \(F\) = factual and causal map;
- \(V\) = value ranking;
- \(\Pi\) = policy recommendation.

For a declared decision boundary \(\Omega\), define a schematic closure operator:

\[
\operatorname{Cl}_{\Omega}(W)
=
\text{the foreseeable commitments and obligations generated by applying }P
\text{ consistently to }F\text{ within }\Omega.
\]

A viewpoint is **constraint-closed relative to \(\Omega\)** when it does not selectively terminate causal or moral reasoning at the point where further consequences become inconvenient.

This is not literal totality. The boundary, uncertainty, time horizon, and excluded variables must be declared.

### 6.2 The opposition checksum

Let \(W_A\) and \(W_B\) be rival viewpoints. Define the opposition checksum of \(B\) against \(A\) as the strongest variable or relation emphasized by \(B\) that:

1. is absent or underweighted in \(A\);
2. materially changes the decision surface;
3. survives factual, causal, source-map, rights, and hostile-control review.

Write it schematically as:

\[
\operatorname{OC}_{B\rightarrow A}
=
\arg\max_{x\in X_B\setminus X_A}
\operatorname{Impact}(x)
\]

subject to validity constraints.

The checksum is not “whatever the opponent cares about.” Falsehood, dehumanization, fabricated evidence, or a demand to violate basic standing does not earn permanent inclusion merely because it is sincerely held.

### 6.3 Constraint-closed compromise

Construct the verified joint constraint set:

\[
\mathcal C^\ast
=
\operatorname{Valid}
\left(
\operatorname{Cl}_{\Omega}(W_A)
\cup
\operatorname{Cl}_{\Omega}(W_B)
\cup
\operatorname{OC}_{B\rightarrow A}
\cup
\operatorname{OC}_{A\rightarrow B}
\right).
\]

A **constraint-closed compromise** is a policy \(\pi^\ast\) selected from the feasible region induced by \(\mathcal C^\ast\), with its unresolved value tradeoffs stated rather than hidden.

The result may be:

- a true overlap;
- a package deal;
- a third policy absent from the original debate;
- one side's proposal after the other side's variables are shown not to survive;
- no permissible compromise.

The central rule is:

> **Each side must carry more of reality before the bargaining starts.**

## 7. Worked case: abortion without erased variables

This example is included because it cleanly exposes the difference between slogan compromise and constraint closure. It does **not** derive a unique abortion policy.

Use the neutral labels **abortion-rights viewpoint** and **abortion-opposition viewpoint**. “Pro-abortion” often misdescribes people who defend legal access without treating abortion as intrinsically good.

### 7.1 Variables commonly emphasized by abortion-rights arguments

- bodily integrity and autonomy;
- health and mortality risk;
- privacy and medical decision-making;
- rape, coercion, and abuse;
- unequal economic and social burdens;
- gestational stage and medical uncertainty;
- miscarriage and emergency-care spillovers;
- unequal surveillance and enforcement;
- the difference between moral concern and state-compelled bodily use.

### 7.2 Variables commonly emphasized by abortion-opposition arguments

- the moral status of developing human life;
- continuity of human development;
- duties toward radically dependent life;
- parental and social responsibility;
- gestational development, viability, and sentience;
- moral injury or regret;
- the claim that autonomy has limits when another morally relevant being is involved.

### 7.3 Closure requirements

An abortion-rights viewpoint does not become coherent merely by setting fetal moral value to zero. It owes an account of why development, stage, dependence, viability, sentience, or prospective personhood do or do not alter moral weight. It must be able to acknowledge:

\[
\text{fetal moral significance}>0
\]

without conceding that this significance always overrides bodily autonomy.

An abortion-opposition viewpoint does not become coherent merely by prohibiting abortion. If public power compels continuation of pregnancy, then the viewpoint inherits downstream obligations concerning:

- prenatal and childbirth care;
- maternal health and disability;
- income loss and employment protection;
- parental leave and childcare;
- infant and child healthcare;
- food and housing security;
- disability support;
- adoption and foster-system capacity;
- costs imposed by compulsory birth;
- burdens of investigation and enforcement.

The exact institutional allocation—state, family, community, insurance, charity, or a mixture—remains debatable. What is not coherent is treating birth as the endpoint of moral responsibility.

The same concern generalizes beyond legally compelled births. If the governing principle is that vulnerable human life is morally important, a consistent closure should track the welfare of children and parents after birth generally rather than only in the politically salient interval before delivery.

### 7.4 The expanded shared object

A joint high-resolution model can include:

\[
\boxed{
\text{developing-life moral weight}>0
\quad\land\quad
\text{bodily autonomy}>0
\quad\land\quad
\text{maternal and child welfare obligations}>0.
}
\]

The remaining dispute concerns:

- empirical facts;
- moral weights;
- gestational thresholds;
- exceptions;
- institutional competence;
- enforceability;
- rights boundaries;
- distribution of burdens.

This is still a hard conflict. But it is a conflict inside a more cohesive total viewpoint rather than a contest between two strategically incomplete worlds.

### 7.5 What this example does not establish

It does not show that every compromise is just, that all moral claims are commensurable, that state welfare is the only support mechanism, or that one exact legal regime follows from the framework.

Its result is methodological:

> **No side earns coherence by tracking one morally vivid variable while discarding the foreseeable persons, burdens, and obligations generated by its own principle.**

## 8. Liberal education as democratic semantic infrastructure

The companion *This Is Water* note proposes a fourth function of liberal education:

> build shared conceptual infrastructure that increases the expected fidelity of perspective transport among differently situated minds.

This acquires a civic role here. A pluralist democracy does not require citizens to share conclusions, but it does require enough common representational infrastructure that they can locate their actual disagreements.

A broad shared education can provide:

- historical cases;
- political and moral vocabularies;
- statistical and causal tools;
- literary simulations of other lives;
- constitutional concepts;
- scientific source discipline;
- analogies that compress recurring structures;
- pointers into accumulated human inquiry.

The democratic target is not ideological uniformity:

\[
\text{shared education}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{shared conclusion}.
\]

It is interoperability:

\[
\text{shared conceptual coverage}
\rightarrow
\text{lower translation cost}
\rightarrow
\text{more precise disagreement}.
\]

The danger is that a common canon can create correlated blind spots. Shared infrastructure must therefore remain revisable, plural, and exposed to omitted perspectives.

## 9. Prior art and novelty boundary

The present synthesis sits near several established literatures.

### 9.1 Voting heuristics and low-information rationality

- Campbell, Converse, Miller, and Stokes analyze party identification, candidate perception, social groups, and issue concepts in *The American Voter*.
- Samuel Popkin's *The Reasoning Voter* models information shortcuts and political symbols.
- Gabriel Lenz's *Follow the Leader?* questions the assumption that voters begin with stable policy views and simply select aligned leaders.
- Kinder and Kalmoe argue that mass partisanship does not imply a uniformly ideological public.

These literatures support the claim that voters use compressed cues and social meanings. They do not by themselves supply the semantic-interoperability or constraint-closure model proposed here.

### 9.2 Moral reframing

Feinberg and Willer show that advocates often argue from their own moral values and that political arguments can become more persuasive when reframed in the audience's moral language.

Constraint closure differs in aim. Moral reframing asks:

> How can I make my existing conclusion legible or persuasive inside your moral basis?

Constraint closure asks:

> Which variable in your basis reveals a genuine omission in mine, and what happens when I permanently incorporate it before deciding what conclusion survives?

Reframing can leave the sender's model unchanged. Constraint closure demands possible model revision.

### 9.3 Deliberation, perspective-taking, and cross-partisan empathy

Research by Muradova, Rossiter and Carlson, Santos et al., and others suggests that perspective-taking, empathy, storytelling, and cross-partisan conversation can improve understanding or reduce animosity under some conditions.

The proposed framework is compatible with that work but more structurally specific about the target: reconstruct the opponent's model, identify the load-bearing residual, validate it, and close one's own model under the resulting constraint.

### 9.4 Integrative complexity

Suedfeld, Tetlock, and later researchers distinguish simple, rigid processing from differentiated and integrated treatment of competing dimensions. Constraint-closed viewpoints are adjacent to integrative complexity but not identical. High verbal complexity can still omit one decisive consequence, and constraint closure can sometimes be expressed simply once the relevant variables are found.

### 9.5 Overlapping consensus and deliberative democracy

Rawlsian overlapping consensus and Habermasian deliberation concern how citizens with different comprehensive doctrines can justify shared political arrangements. The present proposal is compatible with that tradition but operates one layer earlier: whether citizens can faithfully reconstruct the objects and downstream commitments at issue before public justification begins.

### 9.6 Candidate contribution

Academic novelty is **UNVERIFIED**. The repository-level contribution is the synthesis:

1. represent political conflict as transport among private learned bases;
2. treat vibes as low-dimensional candidate/world-model resonance;
3. model parties as persistent labels over changing coalitions and semantic bases;
4. diagnose culture war as identity-coupled codec divergence;
5. require bounded closure under each side's own consequences;
6. add an opposition checksum that must be incorporated if it survives adversarial validation;
7. define real compromise as search over the jointly expanded constraint surface.

The strongest potentially distinctive seam is items 5–7.

## 10. Failure modes and boundaries

### 10.1 False balance

Two sides appearing in public discourse does not imply equal evidential quality. The joint set must pass source and causal review. A fabricated variable does not become load-bearing because a coalition repeats it.

### 10.2 Rights and standing boundaries

A compromise procedure must not treat another person's basic standing as merely one negotiable preference among others. Some positions fail before bargaining because they erase persons, deny equal standing, or demand domination.

The exact rights boundary requires normative theory beyond this dossier.

### 10.3 Power asymmetry

Semantic repair does not neutralize coercive power. A dominant group can demand “understanding” while preserving material control. Every application must track who bears the costs, who can exit, who sets the agenda, and who controls enforcement.

### 10.4 Bad faith

A strategic actor may learn the opponent's basis only to manipulate it. High semantic interoperability can improve propaganda. Understanding must therefore remain separate from ethical routing and truth discipline.

### 10.5 Infinite closure

No policy can include every downstream consequence. Closure must declare:

- scope;
- time horizon;
- affected populations;
- uncertainty;
- threshold for materiality;
- excluded effects and reasons.

### 10.6 Performative complexity

A long list of variables is not automatically a better model. The test is whether each variable changes a prediction, obligation, feasibility condition, or moral ranking.

### 10.7 Compromise fetishism

Some conflicts have no just overlap. The method must be allowed to return:

\[
\boxed{\text{NO FEASIBLE COMPROMISE UNDER THE DECLARED CONSTRAINTS}.}
\]

That is a result, not a failure of civility.

## 11. Empirical and computational program

All measurements below are proposals.

### 11.1 Semantic reconstruction task

Give participant A a structured political argument and ask A to communicate it to B. Measure:

- message length;
- reconstruction accuracy;
- number of repair turns;
- confidence;
- agreement;
- factual accuracy;
- emotional recognition.

Manipulate shared conceptual coverage and analogy availability.

### 11.2 Culture-war codec map

For selected political tokens, collect free-response expansions across groups. Build relational maps rather than word-frequency maps. Test whether the same token invokes divergent causal and moral structures.

Possible tokens:

- freedom;
- equality;
- merit;
- family;
- safety;
- racism;
- religious liberty;
- bodily autonomy;
- law and order.

### 11.3 Opposition-checksum intervention

Before discussion, require each participant to state:

1. the strongest valid variable their own side tracks;
2. the strongest valid variable the other side tracks;
3. one downstream obligation generated by their own principle;
4. one condition that would falsify or narrow their preferred policy.

Compare ordinary discussion against checksum discussion on:

- opponent-model accuracy;
- policy movement;
- discovery of third options;
- affective polarization;
- perceived legitimacy;
- factual learning;
- durability of incorporated variables.

### 11.4 Constraint-closure score

For a bounded policy brief, predeclare a set of downstream consequence classes. Score whether the brief:

- acknowledges each class;
- estimates its direction and uncertainty;
- assigns responsibility;
- states tradeoffs;
- provides a discriminator;
- avoids stopping at a politically convenient boundary.

The score must not become a single truth metric. It is a diagnostic vector.

### 11.5 Historical semantic-basis tomography

Use party platforms, speeches, newspapers, demographic data, election returns, religious affiliation, occupation, region, and educational composition to estimate changing basis weights:

\[
B_j(t)
=
\sum_k w_{kj}(t)b_k(t).
\]

The goal is not to prove a clean party swap but to locate when racial, religious, economic, regional, and status-coded vectors moved on different clocks.

Goldwater's 1964 and 1981 texts provide a useful within-person calibration for semantic drift under the stable token “conservative.”

## 12. Paper contract

The first draft should satisfy the following.

### 12.1 Required claims

- Politics is represented through learned bases, not received as raw propositions.
- Candidate “vibe” can be modeled as perceived coherence between a candidate representation and a voter's world-model.
- Resonance is not truth.
- Parties are carriers whose coalitions and semantic bases change through time.
- Culture war includes codec divergence but cannot be reduced to miscommunication.
- Real compromise requires bounded closure under consequences and validated opposition checksums.
- Liberal education can function as democratic semantic infrastructure.

### 12.2 Required boundaries

- No simple “party switch” story.
- No claim that college credentials equal conceptual coverage.
- No assumption that all perspectives are equally valid.
- No claim that understanding guarantees agreement or morality.
- No requirement to compromise with dehumanization, coercion, or factual fabrication.
- No claim that the abortion example yields one uniquely correct policy.
- No TLICA foundation change.
- Academic novelty remains unverified.

### 12.3 Required adversarial questions

1. Does constraint closure merely rename policy analysis or reflective equilibrium?
2. How is the opposition checksum selected without smuggling in the author's preferred values?
3. Can a viewpoint satisfy closure by listing consequences while refusing to weight them?
4. How are rights boundaries specified without begging the political question?
5. What prevents strategic actors from weaponizing cross-basis understanding?
6. When does semantic interoperability increase coordinated falsehood rather than democratic health?
7. Can the method return no compromise without becoming another rhetorical veto?

## 14. Compact formulation

The full proposal reduces to:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{shared world}
\xrightarrow{\text{different histories}}
\text{different representational bases};\\
&\text{candidate signal}
\xrightarrow{\text{basis-specific decoding}}
\text{vibe/resonance};\\
&\text{identity sorting + basis divergence}
\rightarrow
\text{culture war};\\
&\text{semantic interoperability}
\rightarrow
\text{mutual reconstructability, not agreement};\\
&\text{constraint closure + opposition checksum}
\rightarrow
\text{real compromise search over a larger reality-model}.

\]

The civic principle is:

> **The task is not to make every side think the same thing. It is to make every side carry more of reality.**
