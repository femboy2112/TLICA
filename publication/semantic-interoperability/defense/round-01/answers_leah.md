## Questions (reference — do not answer here; answer in the section below)

**Q1** · *ChatGPT · central claim; motivating observation*
> Think of the clearest real conversation in which both people shared a language and
> seemed to be communicating in good faith, yet the idea did not actually transfer.
> What, in your view, failed?

**Q2** · *ChatGPT · load-bearing invariant; F-003/F-010 gap*
> When you say that an idea has actually transferred from one mind to another, what must
> remain the same despite the two people having different words, experiences, and
> internal representations?

**Q3** · *ChatGPT · C-004/C-007; understanding vs signal echo*
> What observable ability or response would convince you that someone understood an idea
> rather than merely echoed the correct words?

**Q4** · *ChatGPT · C-008; liberal education as semantic infrastructure*
> What does a liberal-arts education contribute to that process that ordinary language
> fluency and the general instruction "think critically" do not?

**Q5** · *ChatGPT · C-009; anti-brainwashing / anti-indoctrination boundary*
> Where, in principle, is the boundary between expanding a person's available modes of
> understanding and training that person to interpret everything through one approved
> mode?

**Q6** · *Claude · native thesis — the "multiple valid answers to what is communication?" demonstration*
> Your thesis answers "what does it mean for two people to communicate?" several
> different ways in a row — each one valid, each one structurally different — and then
> turns to the reader and asks which answer *they* would have given. Set the finished
> paper aside: in your own words, what is that opening move meant to show the reader, and
> why did you choose to *demonstrate* it that way instead of just stating a definition of
> communication up front?

**Q7** · *Claude · canonical note "Semantic interoperability" + native thesis ("shared language, not shared dictionary"); F-001/F-002*
> You say we all share a language but not a *dictionary*, and that the way we attach
> meaning to words is "by analogy to experience." In your own words, what *is* a
> "dictionary" in your account — where does one person's actually live, what is it made
> of, and what does it concretely mean for two people's dictionaries to differ rather
> than just their words?

**Q8** · *Claude · native thesis (understanding vs signal-echo); C-001. (Overlaps Q3 — different pressure: a remembered moment, not a forward test.)*
> You draw a hard line between real understanding and "semantic regurgitation" / "signal
> echo." Forget any formal test for the moment and give me a moment instead: describe
> one time — real or imagined — where you could actually *tell* that someone had
> understood your idea rather than just echoed your words back. What did the
> understanding case have that the echo case was missing?

**Q9** · *Claude · native thesis (the "trust but verify" epistemic core); C-006 / boundary F-008*
> Your thesis rests a lot of weight on "trust but *verify*" — that because only your own
> existence is certain, every message carries baked-in assumptions on both sides. In your
> own words, what does "verify" concretely require of the *listener* — what is a person
> actually *doing* when they verify that they understood, as opposed to just receiving
> the signal and nodding?

**Q10** · *Claude · native thesis (liberal arts; "not about teaching you how to think"); C-008; anti-brainwashing boundary. (Overlaps Q4 — different pressure: mechanism, not contribution.)*
> You say a liberal education is "not about teaching you how to think" but about giving
> someone "the wisdom to understand what is already stated plainly and what needs more
> critical thought." In your own words, what does a liberal education actually *do* to a
> person — by what *mechanism*, not just what outcome — that produces that wisdom? What
> is physically different about how that person now takes in what someone says?
