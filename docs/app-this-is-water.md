# This Is Water

[← Applications overview](applications.md) · [Wiki home](README.md) · Source: [`applications/this_is_water_truth_respecting_choice_v0_1_0.md`](../applications/this_is_water_truth_respecting_choice_v0_1_0.md) · **v0.1.0 · First draft**

> **First draft.** This is a transport bridge, not a theory of Truth or a theory of the
> right thing to do. It borrows David Foster Wallace's 2005 Kenyon commencement address
> ("This Is Water") and his 2003 ZDF interview, and asks a narrow architectural question:
> when does knowing about your own default settings actually reach the moment where it
> could change what you do? Its nine new predictions (P1–P9) are all **UNVERIFIED**. It
> explicitly rejects the idea that understanding the machinery makes anyone good, or that
> understanding a default cancels responsibility for what got routed through it.

---

## What Wallace actually says

The paper reads the delivered Kenyon address carefully — not the cliché version. Its
skeleton, movement by movement: an obvious feature of an environment can be the hardest
one to notice (the fish and the water); a liberal-arts education is really about what
gets thought and how meaning gets built, not raw brainpower; one event can be organized
by incompatible templates and certainty can imprison either side (the Alaskan atheist and
believer); intelligence can deepen a trap rather than escape it; the philosophical
problem gets concrete and mundane under fatigue — a crowded, slow supermarket line;
unlikely counterstories can reopen a closed frame without adding a single new fact;
unconscious "worship" — money, body, power, being seen as smart — quietly organizes what
counts as gain, loss, enough, or threat; there are two freedoms, and the harder one is
attention, awareness, and care rather than preference-satisfaction; and none of this is a
one-time insight — it has to be practiced for life.

> **In plain terms —** Wallace isn't handing out a moral platitude to "be less selfish."
> He's describing a structural problem: your own perspective is so close to you that it's
> invisible, the way water is invisible to a fish. Naming it once doesn't fix it. The work
> is noticing it again, and again, on ordinary tired Tuesdays.

Crucially, the paper is careful about what the speech does and doesn't claim. Wallace's
grocery-store counterstories — the other driver is rushing to the hospital, that person's
carrying real pain you can't see — are explicitly marked by Wallace as *unlikely but
possible*, not as things you should believe. Their job isn't to hand you a nicer fact; it's
to break the premature certainty that your own irritated reading is the only reading
available. Wallace also denies being "the wise fish" who's got it figured out, and he says
outright that the shift takes effort and that some days you won't manage it.

The 2003 ZDF interview supplies the half of the picture the speech only implies: American
commercial and media systems have a structural incentive to court the impulsive,
self-centered, novelty-seeking part of a person, because that's what sells. Wallace
describes a feedback loop — comparison and lack generate demand, purchases and stimulation
offer relief, relief preserves the demand — plus a warning that "freedom of choice" can
mask appetite becoming a felt need, that speed and constant stimulation erode the capacity
for quiet sustained attention (his remote-control example: the choice itself invites
restless scanning rather than presence), that irony can either expose the cage or let you
perform resistance from safely inside it, that serious art can cross the wall of the self
in a way passive entertainment usually doesn't, and that meaningful rebellion tends to be
quiet and unglamorous — consuming less, checking sources, thinking hard before voting —
rather than the dramatic spectacle mass culture is willing to sell you. Wallace repeatedly
qualifies all of this: he doesn't think Americans are uniquely evil, doesn't offer a cure,
and calls his own formulations crude and uncertain.

> **In plain terms —** The water isn't just "how minds naturally work." Some of it is
> manufactured — institutions have found that appetite, insecurity, and distraction are
> profitable to keep active. Noticing your own defaults is necessary, but an environment
> built to keep you from noticing is a second, separate problem.

## What TLICA already had on hand

Before adding anything new, the paper inventories the machinery the rest of the archive
already supplies for this problem.

- **[The three coordinates](three-coordinates.md), κ / φ / ρ**, keep three things from
  collapsing into one: how much real contact is reaching you (κ), how well a content has
  earned its current standing against the tools you can run on it (φ), and how deeply it's
  woven into who you are (ρ). "This resonates with me" can mean high ρ, strong affect, or
  apparent φ — none of which, alone, certifies that the content is *true*.
- **Availability is not the same as exercise.** A person can *have* accurate self-knowledge,
  fully available in principle, and still fail to retrieve it before a moment closes.
  *Free Will* already lists non-confabulatory Mode B reflective availability as one
  condition among six for free choice proper — but availability was never meant to require
  that the reflection actually fires in a given episode.
- **Mode B**, from [the modes of development](modes-of-development.md), is the capacity that
  can take accumulated structure — a habit, a frame, a default — and turn it into an object
  of attention rather than just the water you're swimming in.
- **Live-option construction**, inherited from *Agency Architecture*, is what it takes for
  an alternative to become genuinely selectable rather than merely imaginable.

> **In plain terms —** The archive already had almost all the parts: a way to keep truth,
> identity, and contact separate; a way to say "known but not currently active"; and a way
> to describe building a real alternative rather than just a wish. What was missing was a
> name for the exact moment those parts have to click together in time.

## The new piece: micro-periagoge

The paper's actual contribution is narrow and precisely staged. Call the moment after which
a choice can no longer be revised without opening a whole new decision episode **selection
closure**. The question the paper isolates: how does knowledge about your own architecture
become causally active *before* that closure, early enough to actually change which options
are live?

It names the answer a **present-indexed reorientation event**, or **micro-periagoge**
("turning of the soul," in miniature) — and it only counts if six conditions all hold
together:

- **R1 — Timely activation.** The relevant self-knowledge becomes available *before*
  closure. Insight that arrives after doesn't rescue the completed choice — it can only
  feed the next one.
- **R2 — Actual process-object access.** Reflection has to actually take the *current*
  operative default as its object — not produce a coherent story about it after the fact.
- **R3 — Status preservation.** Fact, memory, inference, imagination, felt signal, source
  attribution, confidence, and value stay distinguished. The original reading stays a
  candidate; nothing gets promoted from possibility to fact without warrant.
- **R4 — Contrastive reopening.** At least one alternative becomes genuinely live —
  representable, evaluable, connected to actually doing it. "I could theoretically react
  differently" doesn't qualify.
- **R5 — Comparative evaluation without capture.** The comparison has to be real, not
  theater — no bypass, preemption, or capture quietly deciding the outcome underneath it.
- **R6 — Selection and implementation.** Something internal actually changes — focus,
  interpretation, epistemic status, or action. Considering an alternative and then snapping
  back unchanged isn't reorientation.

There's a second, developmental route too: **compiled transport**, where earlier online
reorientations and practice have already reshaped the toolkit, cues, habits, or
dispositions, so the person now responds well *without* explicit deliberation in the
moment — and the trained response remains Mode B–accessible under intact conditions if
examined. This is compatible with ordinary skilled action; it just isn't a fresh
reorientation event each time.

> **In plain terms —** Just knowing a fact about your own patterns doesn't automatically
> help. It has to (1) show up in time, (2) actually grab the thing that's currently running
> the show, (3) keep your facts, guesses, and feelings sorted, (4) open a real alternative,
> (5) let you genuinely weigh it rather than fake-weigh it, and (6) actually change
> something. Miss any one of the six, and self-knowledge just sits there, true and useless.

The paper is blunt about the core result being deliberately small: self-knowledge can
enlarge agency only when it becomes causally active before selection, or has already been
compiled into what structures selection. This is a claim about *transport*, not a theorem
that self-knowledge produces truth, virtue, or the uniquely right action.

## Truth-respecting choice — and its guardrails

Because TLICA doesn't yet have a general theory connecting a mental content to whether it's
actually true, the paper is careful to say **"truth-respecting," not "true."** A
reorientation is truth-respecting only if it clears a conjunctive set of epistemic guardrails (TR1–TR5, a separate
checklist from the R1–R6 micro-periagoge conditions above — those decide whether a
reorientation *happens* at all, these whether it's *truth-respecting*): it stays answerable to observed evidence (TR1); keeps fact, inference,
possibility, and unknown distinct rather than borrowing confidence from feeling (TR2);
tracks its own best-supported source honestly, marked as a hypothesis rather than direct
access to the truth (TR3); stays corrigible — open to the observations that would overturn
it (TR4); and doesn't let identity-fit, resonance, or preservation-priority stand in for
evidence (TR5). None of these can be traded off against a high score on the others — a
warm story that can't be challenged is, the paper says plainly, "another cave."

A separate, additional set of **ethical and action-admissibility guardrails** governs
whether an action is defensible at all: whether the other person's standing is preserved
rather than reduced to a role; whether an accurate model is actually routed into care
rather than used coldly; feasibility and safety; how uncertainty should weigh against
urgency; whether a person's own boundaries survive the exercise of generosity toward
someone else; and — pointedly — whether resistance or exit, not reframing, is the honest
response. The paper is explicit that TLICA doesn't derive these ethical guardrails from
κ, φ, and ρ; they're proposed at the application level, open to independent contest.

Given all that, the strongest thing the paper will say about an outcome is a **right-path
candidate**: a genuinely live, owned, source-disciplined, truth-respecting option that
passes the paper's provisional ethical and safety screen — not "the" right path, and not
proof that the candidate is objectively correct.

> **In plain terms —** "It felt right" is never, by itself, evidence that it *was* right.
> The paper builds a checklist a possible reorientation has to pass — stay honest about
> what you actually know, stay checkable, don't let feelings do evidence's job — and even
> a reorientation that clears every box only earns the modest label "a defensible
> candidate," not "the truth" or "the good."

The paper also revisits *The Self-Applied Architecture*'s own internal tension about
resonance — one part of that paper treats resonance as marking what's true, a later part
correctly insists resonance is high-sensitivity, low-specificity, and only becomes usable
after a disciplined dig into its provenance. This paper says the later, more disciplined
rule has to govern: resonance can nominate something for examination, reveal that
something matters, or help choose among options that have *already* passed the evidence
and ethics screens — it can never verify itself, prove an identity claim, or certify
rightness.

## A fallible moral standpoint

The paper is emphatic, in its own §2.2, that its author is not offering herself as a moral
exemplar. Her stated position is close to the opposite: "I am not saying I am some beacon
of moral value. I constantly fail at being good, but I try, and when I fail it is mostly
due to what Wallace talks about." The paper treats that "mostly due" clause as a leading
hypothesis about her own failures to test — not a validated causal explanation, and
certainly not an excuse. It explicitly rejects the idea that understanding a default
automatically cancels responsibility for conduct routed through it: mechanism, avoidability,
culpability, repair, and prevention are different questions, and a causal account may bear
on culpability without settling it. The paper even names this failure mode directly —
"explanation as exculpation" — and warns that if the theory is used only after the fact to
excuse an unchanged pattern, it has become another device for protecting a self-image
rather than a genuine tool for reorientation.

Its working moral aim, borrowed from earlier drafts, is deliberately modest: **"set up to
be able to be good,"** not moral purity or a guarantee of harmlessness. Failure is treated
as part of the data, not as disqualifying.

> **In plain terms —** This paper doesn't say "learn the theory and become good." It says
> something much smaller and, the author argues, more honest: understanding your own
> architecture can occasionally open a door in time — it does not walk you through it, and
> it never excuses you for the doors you didn't open.
