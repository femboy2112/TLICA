# Modes of Development

[← Wiki home](README.md) · Source: [File 2, §4](../foundation/2_access_and_development.md)

---

The [cogito](the-cogito.md) is anchored from the start, but there is **no fully-formed
self at that moment**. The lived self *emerges*, through three modes of development.
[Access to Intrinsic Structure](access-to-intrinsic-structure.md) is about building the
reasoning toolkit; this page is about building the *self*.

The cogito gives you a bare "I am" — a formal anchor present from the very first instant
(the theory writes this instant **$t = 0_{T_m}$**, just shorthand for "time zero, on the
modeling-I's own clock"). But "I am" is not yet "I am *this person*, with these
attachments, these edges, this inner life." That richer, lived self has to be *built*. The
three modes below are the three ways building happens.

> **In plain terms —** Being conscious and being *somebody* are two different things. You
> arrive conscious; you become somebody. This page is the construction manual for the
> "becoming somebody" part.

## Modes are response-types, not predictors

This framing matters. The three modes are **categories of developmental response** to an
encounter — not a rule for *which* response will fire. The theory says what each mode
*does* if it operates; it does **not** predict which one operates for a given encounter
(that depends on substrate, history, environment, and chance it doesn't formalize). The
analogy: thermodynamics doesn't predict which way one molecule jumps, but it predicts
what kinds of motion are possible and the statistics over many. (See exclusion 17 in
[Predictions & Exclusions](predictions-and-exclusions.md).)

Put concretely: meet a stranger at a party and you might bristle at them (Mode A), warm to
them and adopt their slang within a week (Mode C), or have their offhand question
("why do you always do that?") send you into a spiral of self-examination (Mode B). The
theory tells you what each of those reactions *accomplishes* in building your self. It
flatly declines to tell you, in advance, which reaction you'll have to *this* stranger.

> **In plain terms —** These are three things that can happen to you when you bump into the
> world, not three predictions about what *will* happen. The theory describes the machinery;
> it does not pretend to read your fortune.

## Mode A — Adversarial differentiation

When you encounter something "other" enough, it registers as **discrepant** — foreign,
out of place, wrong-from-here. That registration **draws a boundary**. Mode A is the
mechanism by which an I/not-I distinction exists at all; without it there's no separable
self to build on.

The source unpacks Mode A into **three parts that fire together**:

1. **The otherness-trigger.** An automatic registration that something is foreign,
   wrong-from-here, out of place. You don't *decide* it's discrepant; the registration
   happens to you when the conditions are met.
2. **The prerogative of consistency (frame-privileging).** The discrepancy isn't measured
   against nothing — it's measured against *you*. Your own frame is the silent standard,
   and the other thing counts as "off" precisely because it clashes with *your* frame.
3. **The organic creation of the I.** Out of that boundary-drawing pressure, a
   *differentiated agent* — a separable you — actually comes into being. The cogito anchor
   was already there, but the lived capacity to act *as a distinct someone* switches on
   here.

Built into Mode A is the **prerogative of consistency** — abbreviated **PtCns** in the
theory, and in plain terms just this: *your frame is the home team*. The discrepancy isn't
registered neutrally — *your own frame is the implicit standard* against which the other
thing counts as discrepant. This is not a claim that your frame is *correct* (morally or
factually); it's a claim that your structure is built to *defend its own consistency*. You
don't pause to think "maybe I'm the one who's wrong here" — the very registering of the
other thing as "wrong" *is* your frame asserting itself as the standard. (See
[The Two Prerogatives](the-two-prerogatives.md).)

> **In plain terms —** Mode A is the "ew, not me" reflex — and that reflex is what carves
> out a "me" to begin with. The wall it builds *is* the self's first edge.

> **From the inside —** A flavor of food you find genuinely repellent; a stranger's
> opinion that makes you wince before you've even argued with it; walking into a room where
> the vibe is just *wrong*. In each case there's an instant, pre-verbal "that is not me" —
> and notice that you never doubt yourself in that instant. You don't wonder if your taste
> is mistaken; the other thing is simply *off*. That instant is your frame quietly crowning
> itself the standard. That is PtCns.

**Mode A produces the boundary.**

## Mode C — Positive identification

When you encounter something that registers as *continuous with you* — comforting, close,
already-yours — it gets **folded inside** the boundary as part of the (proto-)self.

This is the opposite move to Mode A. Mode A meets the foreign and pushes it out; Mode C
meets the kindred and pulls it in. And it's the *dominant* mode early on: before there's a
firm boundary to keep anything out, the immediate world — body parts, caregivers, comfort
objects, food — tends to get swept inside and treated as continuous with the self.

The diagnostic case: take candy from a baby and the baby grieves intensely — not as
denial of a wanted object, but as **loss of self**. The candy had been positively
identified as part of the proto-self, before a firm boundary existed to exclude it. The
adult, in whom Mode A has done its work, experiences the same loss as loss-of-*thing*.
The difference between the baby's howl and the adult's mild annoyance is not the size of
the candy — it's whether a boundary exists that places the candy *outside* the self.

> **In plain terms —** Mode C is the "this is part of me now" move. It's how a blanket, a
> parent, a song, or a hometown stops being mere *stuff out there* and becomes *yours*,
> woven into who you are.

> **From the inside —** Think of losing something that wasn't expensive but gutted you
> anyway — a worn-out hoodie, a dead pet's collar, a friendship that ended. The grief is
> out of all proportion to the *object*, and that's the tell: Mode C had folded it inside,
> so its loss was felt as a piece of *you* being torn out, not a possession being misplaced.

**Mode C populates the inside.** Most of the lived self is built this way.

This is also where TLICA's notion of identity-correlation gets its grip. Mode C is the
engine that raises **ρ (identity-correlation)** — in plain terms, *how much of you is bound
up in a thing; the stuff whose loss would actually change who you are.* The candy has high
ρ for the baby and low ρ for the adult, and that single difference is the whole story of
why one grieves and the other shrugs. (See how this builds *love* in
[Differentiated Affect](app-differentiated-affect.md).)

## Mode B — Reflexive meta-reasoning

You turn attention onto your **own structure** — the cogito-as-act actualizing. Mode B
**can't run at the very beginning**: there has to be *something accumulated* to reflect
on. Once Mode A has drawn a boundary and Mode C has populated some structure, reflexive
attention has material to work on, and it **refines the whole**.

Where Modes A and C point *outward* (at the not-I and decide: keep out, or fold in),
Mode B points *inward* — it takes *you* as its object. It's reason turned back on the
reasoner. And it's necessarily late: you can't reflect on yourself before there's a self
there to reflect on. This is the theory's "developmental escalator" showing up in
self-knowledge — you have to *be* somebody before you can *examine* being somebody.

Two sub-types by trigger:

- **Internally-triggered** ("wait — what am I doing?" arising unprompted). You simply
  *happen upon* the reflexive move — a quiet moment of self-recognition, an introspective
  shift that no one and nothing outside you set off.
- **Externally-triggered** (a question, a work of art, a vast landscape that throws your
  own position into relief). Something *out there* sparks it — but the *content* of the
  event is still *you*. The mountaintop doesn't teach you about mountains; it makes your
  own smallness suddenly visible. The blunt question "why do you always do that?" doesn't
  inform you about the asker; it holds up a mirror.

Whether any given encounter sparks Mode B is **stochastic, with environment-shaped odds** —
"stochastic" just means *chancy, not guaranteed*, and "environment-shaped odds" means *the
surroundings load the dice*. Settings rich in reflection-prompting stimuli (art, puzzles,
philosophical talk, perspective-shifting vistas) raise the per-encounter probability;
barren settings lower it. The roll is still a roll — but the room you're in tilts the
table.

> **In plain terms —** Mode B is the "wait... what am I *doing*?" move — the mind catching
> itself in the act. It can bubble up from nowhere, or get knocked loose by a question, a
> painting, or standing somewhere that makes you feel tiny.

> **From the inside —** That jolt on a mountain ridge when the trees below look like ants
> and you abruptly feel the *scale* of what you're standing inside — and, riding underneath
> it, a small humbling. The theory notes this single experience is actually doing two jobs
> at once: refining your sense of self (Mode B) *and* installing a new reasoning tool about
> your own position (self-encounter, over on the [reasoning-toolkit](access-to-intrinsic-structure.md)
> page). One event, two ledgers.

On *when* Mode B switches on across a population, the theory commits to three structural
constraints — **zero at the very start, non-zero later, varying between minds** — but
**deliberately does not fix a distributional shape** (bell curve, log-normal, etc. are
left open as an empirical question). In plain terms: *no baby is born self-reflective; most
people get there eventually; and the age varies a lot.* A bell curve is the intuitive guess
— most people around some typical age, a few early, a few late — but the theory refuses to
*insist* on that shape, treating it as a measurement for developmental psychology to settle,
not something it can decree from first principles.

> **In plain terms —** Three firm commitments — you start with zero self-reflection, you
> grow into some, and the timing differs person to person — and one honest shrug about the
> exact statistical curve. The theory holds the line it can defend and leaves the rest to
> data.

## The three orders of imprinting

Self-building doesn't happen in a vacuum: every encounter leaves a mark — an **imprint** —
on you. The source distinguishes **three kinds of imprinting**, three different sorts of
mark an encounter can leave:

- **Contact imprinting (κ-changes).** **κ (kappa)** — in plain terms, *raw contact: how
  much a thing has actually touched your attention, how present it's been.* This is the
  bare fact of exposure, the groove worn by repeated encounter.
- **Content imprinting (ρ-changes).** Changes to **ρ (identity-correlation)** — *how much
  of you is bound up in the thing.* This is the imprint that matters for the self: it's
  what Mode C is doing when it folds something inside, and what Mode A guards when it keeps
  something out.
- **Verification-tool imprinting.** The marks that build your *reasoning* toolkit rather
  than your *self* — picking up object-permanence, cause-and-effect, and the rest. This is
  the focus of the [Access to Intrinsic Structure](access-to-intrinsic-structure.md) page,
  not this one, but it's the third of the trio.

> **In plain terms —** Three kinds of dent the world can leave in you: *how much it touched
> you* (κ), *how much of you it became* (ρ), and *what it taught you to reason with*
> (verification tools). This page is mostly about the middle one — the dents that change who
> you are.

## Early development happens *to* you

A consequence the theory takes seriously: through the earliest phases, the I has **no
intentional capacity to direct its own development**. Mode A firings are triggered by what
the world presents; Mode C identifications are handed to you by your immediate
environment; Mode B isn't online yet. There is no self that pre-exists its own
development to steer it.

The logic is airtight and a little vertiginous: the *you* that could choose how to grow has
to *exist first* — but that you is the very thing the growing *produces*. You cannot steer
a process whose job is to build the steerer. So the earliest version of you is assembled by
forces you neither chose nor controlled: a substrate (the body and brain you were handed)
and an environment (the people, objects, and accidents around you).

So **early variation between people is not anyone's choice** — it reflects a substrate
they didn't pick and an environment they didn't control. This grounds the theory's
**descriptive, non-moralizing** stance toward patterns formed in that window, including
many later classed as mental illness. See [Profile-Shape Disturbances](profile-shape-disturbances.md).

> **In plain terms —** Nobody authored their own beginning. The early self gets built by
> things you had no hand in choosing — which is exactly why the theory refuses to *blame*
> anyone for how their early self came out.

## Typical progression

- **Phase 0** (newborn): bare cogito; boundary undrawn; Mode C dominant by default;
  Mode A not yet firing; Mode B impossible.
- **Phase 1**: encounters with distant-enough not-I start firing Mode A; the boundary
  forms; Mode C continues; Mode B may become possible toward the end.
- **Phase 2**: A and C run continuously; the boundary sharpens; Mode B comes online at
  its typical onset.
- **Phase 3** (mature): all three operate; the I can deliberately seek out
  reflection-rich environments.

These are *typical* patterns. Atypical trajectories are statistical outliers, not
failures of the architecture.

> **In plain terms —** The usual arc: first you're a sponge soaking up whatever's near
> (Phase 0), then you start drawing your own edges (Phase 1), then both run at full tilt and
> you wake up to yourself (Phase 2), and finally you can deliberately go *seek* the things
> that make you grow (Phase 3). Off-schedule lives aren't broken — they're just rarer rolls.
