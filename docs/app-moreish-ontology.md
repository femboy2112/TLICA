## The one-line idea

Some worldviews are **moreish** — they season their own consequences, so that living inside them
makes them look *more* correct, not less. This paper takes one such worldview apart. Its thesis is
a single sentence:

> **A sensor may reveal a real truth without being entitled to become the global policy.**

Everything else is that sentence, worked out through three characters and a little control theory.

> **In plain terms —** a smoke detector is great at detecting smoke. It should *not* be allowed to
> run the whole house — deciding when you eat, sleep, or leave. The paper is about what goes wrong
> when a genuinely useful alarm gets promoted to the thing in charge.

## The three sensors

The failure mode is built from three intuitions, each of which is *right about something* — and
each is dramatized as an exaggerated character.

- **Underground Super Hans — the epistemic sensor.** Warn him the consequences will pile up and he
  just shrugs: *"That sounds suspiciously like a model, mate."* And he isn't wrong — he detects
  institutional nonsense, overconfident forecasts, and scripts that demand huge sacrifice now for a
  vaguely-specified later. Genuinely useful. His failure: promoted to **governor**, his skepticism
  stops merely auditing plans and starts governing how much the future is allowed to matter *at all*.
- **Pavlov's Veruca — the reward sensor.** She wants it *now* — *"a life whose value exists only in
  projection is not presently a valuable life."* She's asking whether the system is actually
  *receiving* reward, not just being trained to chase deferred symbols — and she has a point: a life
  of endless postponement (happy *after* graduation, rested *after* the next deadline) may never
  arrive. Her failure: promoted to **head of capital allocation**, immediate reinforcement conditions
  behavior before the slow consequences are felt.
- **The Jesus / WWJD ideal — the moral sensor.** *"Bear ye one another's burdens."* You don't have to
  hold the theology to be true for that phrasing to land — it arrives pre-loaded with the moral gravity
  of the figure attached to it, and that inherited weight is exactly the token in play. Take the ideal
  by its *shape* — not as evidence the figure was real or right, but as the moral silhouette actually
  absorbed — and it distills to a question that won't quite leave you alone: *"Am I protecting myself by
  making someone else carry a cost I could reasonably help carry?"* A real and valuable check on
  selfishness. Its failure: promoted to a **global error-cancelling actuator**, it tries to drive the
  harm you cause toward zero — and since exact moral zero is unobservable, *the self becomes the most
  available place to dump the cost.*

> **In plain terms —** three good instincts — "stay skeptical," "make sure life is actually worth
> living now," "don't make other people pay for you." Each is a fine *advisor*. The trouble starts
> when any one of them is handed the steering wheel.

## The core error: sensor promoted to governor

The deep mistake is **not** hedonism, rebellion, laziness, religion, or self-sacrifice. It is
**scope erasure during compilation**: a truth that holds *under some conditions* gets compiled into
a rule that runs *everywhere*.

- "Attachment can produce suffering" ⇏ "minimize attachment globally."
- "Self-sacrifice can be exemplary" ⇏ "assign the self near-zero weight."
- "Models are corrigible" ⇏ "future models deserve almost no authority."

Each arrow's left side is a real, locally-earned observation. Each right side is an over-broad
policy that does not follow.

> **In plain terms —** noticing that fire is hot is true. Concluding "therefore never go near
> warmth again" is the bug. The observation was fine; the *rule compiled from it* was way too big.

## The Greedy Integral Problem

Here is the little bit of control theory, kept honest as a *schematic*. Suppose a meaningful life
is a sum of moment-by-moment value, $J=\int_0^T u(t)\,dt$. The tempting move is to maximize $u(t)$
at every instant — the **greedy policy**. That works *only if the moments are independent*. But a
life is a dynamical system: today's action changes the state you wake up in tomorrow, and so
changes which futures are even reachable. So:

> **Maximizing the integrand locally does not maximize the integral when the integrand is
> dynamically coupled through state.**

Hans (distrust the projected future) and Veruca (reward the present) together produce a controller
that says *"given the world as it currently exists, choose the most livable state now"* — while the
world that "currently exists" tomorrow was partly built by today. **The controller keeps inheriting
its own output.**

> **In plain terms —** eating the marshmallow is locally optimal every single time. The problem is
> that each choice quietly edits the menu you'll face next. Optimizing each bite is not the same as
> optimizing the meal, once the bites change what's left on the plate.

## Why the future keeps losing the argument

The paper uses one piece of TLICA to explain why this feels so *reasonable* from the inside. The
present is the live [imprinting interface](the-self-in-motion.md); the future is available only
through **Mode-B projection** — simulations of possible imprinting, not already-imprinted actuality.
So *Future-You* matters causally but shows up only as a model. That creates an asymmetry:

> The future is **epistemically weak** and **causally real**. That distinction is the hinge.

The future keeps trying to speak up — *"if you keep doing this, the consequences accumulate"* — and
Hans, from the corner, deflects it every time: *"That sounds suspiciously like a model, mate."* He's
formally correct: it *is* a model. That correctness is exactly what makes it catastrophic — the
consequences land whether or not he grants the projection any authority. Present discomfort is vivid
and contact-rich; future constraint is effortful, uncertain, simulation-rich — and if building those
simulations is exactly what feels expensive and "Underground-inducing," the present wins by default.
The mistake is not refusing exhausting planning; it's treating *model uncertainty* as if it weakened
the *causal coupling* itself.

> **In plain terms —** tomorrow-you is real and will absolutely inherit today's mess — but tomorrow-
> you only exists right now as a fuzzy simulation, while today's discomfort is right here in HD. Of
> course the vivid thing wins. That doesn't make it right.

## The epistemic ratchet

This is the paper's sharpest new mechanism, and the one that explains how someone can keep hurting
themselves while feeling *disciplined*. The way these truths were acquired was a kind of honest
empiricism: an admired thinker supplies a candidate ("Jesus seemed to notice something about
sacrifice"; "the Buddha seemed to notice something about attachment"), you *probe your own lived
experience* for the pattern, and if you feel and notice it, you internalize it. Good so far.

The bug is that the update law became **asymmetric**:

- Felt **resonance** could *install* a rule (candidate + felt recurrence → internalized).
- But felt **suffering** could not easily *uninstall* it — because "pain while following the rule"
  does not logically **refute** the rule.

That last observation is *true*. Its use as a controller is catastrophic. The system learns from
lived resonance and then refuses to learn symmetrically from lived damage:

> **Phenomenology can canonize a rule, but phenomenology cannot easily de-canonize it.**

The missing distinction is between a proposition's **truth** and a policy's **viability**: your
distress can be weak evidence about whether an idea is *true* while being *strong* evidence about
what running that policy is doing to the organism. Both of these can hold at once — `pain ⇏ the
theorem is false`, and yet it is a disaster to conclude `pain ⇏ change the policy`.

> **In plain terms —** it's a one-way ratchet. Feelings were allowed to *add* beliefs but not to
> *remove* them, because "this hurts" isn't a logical disproof. So the pain got filed as a
> *consequence of being right* instead of a signal to stop. The fix is to let distress work as
> telemetry — "this controller is hurting me" — even when it isn't a refutation.

## Why it's *moreish* — the self-seasoning loop

The Hans-state pays out immediately: reframing a constraint as *"I refuse to grant this authority
over me"* buys agency, a sense of seeing-through-it-all, relief from projected obligation, and —
crucially — **comedy**. Once suffering becomes ridiculous it becomes *usable*: story, bit, material.
And that is where it turns self-reinforcing:

> constraint → Hans frame → felt liberation → less future-oriented correction → more constraint →
> stronger evidence for Hans.

The frame produces the very conditions that make the frame look correct. It is not merely
self-sealing; it is **self-seasoning** — the consequences improve the flavor. *This ontology is
really moreish.* And that is precisely why it needs a governor.

> **In plain terms —** the worldview is delicious because every bad outcome it causes becomes fresh
> proof that it was right all along, plus a good joke. That's exactly what makes it so hard to put
> down — and exactly why you can't let it run unsupervised.

## The corrective — demote, don't kill

The repair is **not** to become respectable, cynical, or to abandon sacrifice. All three sensors
stay. The fix is an **authority architecture**: keep the sensors, deny any one of them sole command.

- **Hans is a sensor, not a governor** — keep him detecting fraud and overconfident forecasts.
- **Veruca is a reward sensor, not head of capital allocation** — keep her insisting life be
  inhabitable now.
- **WWJD is a moral probe, not root access** — keep the question *"am I making someone else pay a
  cost I could carry?"* without concluding *"therefore I barely matter."*

The constructive object is an **adaptive feedback policy** rather than a predicted-future plan. A
schematic version maximizes present self-congruent value **plus** future option value **minus**
irreversible downside — and its crucial quantity is *option value*: not "suffer now so Future-You
enjoys later" (easy to reject) but "don't gratuitously delete moves from Future-You's action space."
Money, mobility, skills, sleep, relationships all matter partly because they **preserve options** —
freedom for an *unknown* future, no worship of a specific one. And, pointedly: preserving your own
option set is not automatically selfish — **the self is one of the morally relevant systems whose
future capacities enter the world.**

> **In plain terms —** you don't fire the smoke detector; you take it off the throne. Keep all three
> alarms, let none of them run the house, and steer by a simple rule of thumb — *"is this move
> closing a door I might need?"* — instead of trying to compute the whole future or letting the
> loudest instinct win.

## The dignity of the frame

The paper is autobiographical, and it is deliberately **not** ashamed of the substrate facts it
draws on. That posture is itself an instance of the theory: TLICA holds that a self is *anchored to*
a perspective and is **not identical to** its contents, its history, or its substrate. Having a
history, or a diagnosis, or a failure mode is a **fact of the substrate** — not a verdict on the
person's worth, and not the person. Reading one's own broken controller openly, and repairing it, is
the theory used on its author in good faith.

> **In plain terms —** "I built and ran a bad controller, and here is exactly how" is a report about
> a mechanism, not a confession of being worthless. The whole point of the architecture is that you
> are the one doing the reading — never merely the thing being read.
