# Differentiated Affect

[← Applications overview](applications.md) · [Wiki home](README.md) · Source: [`applications/differentiated_affect_v1_0_2.md`](../applications/differentiated_affect_v1_0_2.md) · **Layer 7 · v1.0.2 · Complete**

---

The richest of the complete papers. Its claim: **emotions are configurations of the self
and its operators** — extension, loss, threat, reflexive evaluation, boundary operation,
frame-expansion, substrate perturbation. Name an emotion and the paper asks: *what is this
doing to the [maximally-self-defined I](the-two-prerogatives.md), and through which
operator?* The payoff is that affect becomes classifiable **by structural operators rather
than by its ordinary labels.**

A few terms recur, so here are the plain-language handles up front:

- **the maximally-self-defined I** — the whole of *who you are* as the architecture models it: not just your body, but everything you've taken in as part of yourself. When the paper says an emotion "perturbs the maximally-self-defined I," read it as *something is happening to you, at the level of who you are.*
- **ρ (identity-correlation)** — how much of *you* is bound up in a thing; the stuff whose loss would actually change who you are. High ρ for your sister means losing her would dent your sense of self; high ρ for your phone usually just means inconvenience.
- **Π (the preservation-ranking)** — your internal priority list of what must be kept safe. Things near the top are what you'd protect first when something has to give.
- **PCE (Prerogative of Continued Existence)** — the built-in pull toward *staying yourself, continuing on*. It's what's operating when you flinch from a threat, plan for the future, or refuse to let go of something that matters.
- **PtCns (Prerogative of Consistency)** — the built-in pull toward *staying coherent, hanging together as one person*. It's what keeps you feeling like a single self rather than a pile of contradictions.
- **Mode A / Mode B / Mode C** — three [ways the self develops](modes-of-development.md): Mode A pushes something *away* (differentiation, "that's not me"), Mode B turns attention *inward or forward* (reflection, imagining futures), Mode C pulls something *in* ("that's part of me now").

> **In plain terms —** an emotion isn't a thing you *have*; it's a particular shape your *self* is in. Love, grief, fear, and anger are different things happening to who-you-are, and the paper's whole trick is to tell them apart by what each one is *doing* rather than by what it's called.

## The method: start from love

Rather than enumerate emotions, the paper defines **one** precisely and derives the rest as
variations of it. That one is **love**, defined structurally by three conditions:

1. The self has [extended](modes-of-development.md) (via Mode C) to include another, **X**,
   as a *constitutive extension* — X is *inside* the self, so that losing X is represented
   as damage to the self. (High [ρ](three-coordinates.md) is necessary but not enough; the
   load-bearing feature is *topological* — X is part of you, not merely strongly linked to
   you.)
2. The [prerogative of consistency](the-two-prerogatives.md) has held the self's frame
   together across the extension (you remain "the one doing the loving," not dissolved into X).
3. X stays in the constitutive range under ordinary fluctuation of mood, contact, and
   certainty.

Each condition is doing specific work, so it's worth unfolding them.

**Condition 1 — X is *inside* you, not just *close* to you.** The paper is careful here: it is not enough to care about someone a lot. A teammate can be lightly identified-with; a close friend can sit very high on your priority list and still be, structurally, *someone else.* Love is when Mode C has gone *all the way* — X has become a part of the self such that **preserving X just is preserving you**, in the action-relevant sense. The test isn't "how strongly do I feel?" but "would losing this person be a *reduction of me*, a piece torn out?" That is what "constitutive" means and why high ρ alone doesn't suffice: ρ measures *strength of link*; love is about *topology* — whether X is on the inside of the boundary or the outside.

> **In plain terms —** the difference between really loving someone and merely being very attached is the difference between "if they were gone, *part of me* would be gone" and "if they were gone, I'd lose something *I have*." Love is when they've crossed from things-you-have to parts-of-you.

**Condition 2 — you survive the merger.** Mode C is always held in check by PtCns, the pull to stay coherent. In love this check is pushed to its limit: you take someone *into* the self without *dissolving into them*. You are still the one doing the loving. When PtCns holds, you remain a distinct, coherent person who now contains the beloved. When PtCns *fails* — when the merger goes past the point where you can stay coherent — you get the dissolution pathologies (obsession, codependency, cultic merger): not deeper love, but **loss of the self that loved.**

> **From the inside —** healthy love feels like *"they're part of me and I'm still me."* Dissolution feels like *"I don't exist apart from them anymore"* — and that, the paper insists, is not love turned up to maximum but the self quietly going out.

**Condition 3 — it holds steady through the small stuff.** Once X is constitutive, X inherits the self's own preservation-priority, and that priority doesn't wobble with the daily weather. The paper makes this concrete: you don't stop loving someone when you're **apart from them (low κ — contact dropping), when you can't articulate *why* you love them (φ undefined — no verbalizable reason to point at), or during a stretch of just feeling distant.** None of these change the underlying *topology*; they're surface variation. This "invariance" is the structural signature that separates love from its look-alikes:

- **κ (contact)** — *infatuation* has the high priority but collapses the moment contact stops; it needs constant feeding.
- **φ (source-pathway, the articulable "why")** — *admiration* collapses on disillusionment; it needed the reasons to stay stateable.
- **ρ (identity-correlation)** — *identification* collapses when *you* reorganize and the thing no longer fits.

> **In plain terms —** love is the one that *survives the gaps*. Distance, dry spells, not being able to say why — none of it touches it, because the connection isn't held up by feelings, contact, or reasons. Those can all sag and the love stays. That stubborn steadiness *is* what tells you it was love and not a crush.

A lot follows immediately: self-sacrifice for a loved one stops being paradoxical (the child
*is* part of you — dying for them isn't valuing them *over* yourself, it's preserving the larger you); "you complete me" stops being metaphor; love can survive the beloved
changing, and can be one-sided ([unrequited love](three-coordinates.md) is real in *your* architecture even if unmirrored in theirs), or directed at a country or a craft. And love can end **only
three ways** — active [Mode A](modes-of-development.md) differentiation (the real "falling
out of love," usually an accumulation of tiny differentiating updates), a wholesale
reorganization of the self, or death. Ordinary *fading* doesn't dissolve it.

> When the structure shifts before you can explain why, you get **recognition-lag** — the
> profile changes first, the conscious story catches up later. It's the architecture's
> account of "everyone could see it but you."

> **In plain terms —** you can stop loving someone, but never by *drifting*. Either you actively push them out of yourself one small disappointment at a time (and only notice you've done it once it's already done), or you become a different person who doesn't have room for them, or one of you dies. Mere fading isn't on the menu.

## How the rest is derived: love, then operations on the profile

Here is the engine of the whole paper. Once love is pinned down as *"X is a constitutive part of you,"* every other emotion can be written as an **operation on that configuration** — something done *to* the extension, the boundary, the self, or the projected future. You don't define grief from scratch; you ask: *what happens to a constitutive extension when it's lost?* You don't define fear from scratch; you ask: *what happens when damage to the self is projected ahead?* The clusters below are the catalogue of operations. Four worked examples make the method visible.

### Grief — the extension *removed*

**Grief is the loss of a constitutive extension.** Take love's configuration (X inside you) and delete X from the world. The self has now lost a *part of itself*, and the remaining structure has to reorganize around the hole.

This is why the paper says the shaking is **literal**, not figurative. The structural object whose preservation *defined* your continuation has been damaged. "Part of me died when they died," "I am not who I was," "the world is different now" — these are accurate inside-reports of a self that has a piece missing and is rebuilding.

The classic grief "stages" then become not a fixed sequence but different *profile-dynamics* of that rebuild:

- **Shock** — the self is still running *as if* X were present; the loss hasn't propagated through yet.
- **Denial** — ρ for the lost extension is being *held up* against a world that no longer supports it. Not pathology — the self's natural inertia in updating a constitutive position.
- **Anger** — Mode A firing: differentiating against the circumstances, the doctors, fate, even the beloved for leaving. Pushing-away that lets the rebuild start.
- **Bargaining** — PCE running in *counterfactual mode*, hunting a path back to the pre-loss self. When no such path exists, it fades.
- **Depression** — the self at its most damaged mid-rebuild: Π destabilized, no stable priority-ranking over the broken configuration, affective range collapsed, the future foreclosed.
- **Acceptance** — the self has restabilized. The beloved's role is *transformed* — either reduced out of the action-relevant self, or kept as a **continuing-bond extension** (memory, vow, lineage, "sacred absence"). "I still love my dead child" can be a *stable* state, not unfinished grief.

> **In plain terms —** grief takes time because it's *real construction work*, not "processing feelings." Every plan, habit, and reflex that assumed the person would still be there has to be rebuilt by hand. You can't shortcut it any more than you can shortcut rebuilding a house; skip the work and you're left standing in an unstable self.

> **From the inside —** the flatness, the missing energy, the foreclosed future of deep grief is what it feels like *from inside* a self that is structurally incomplete and hasn't found its new shape yet.

### Fear — damage to the self, *projected ahead*

**Fear is PCE-projection toward a specific projected damage.** Now take the self and run it forward in time to a state where it gets hurt. PCE (the pull to keep existing) reacts to that *projected* harm the way it would react to *present* harm — and orients action to prevent, escape, or defend.

The defining feature is **focal anchoring**: the threat is *specific*, the timing is *anticipated* (not yet, but could be), and an *action-pathway* is available or being built. That triad is what makes it fear rather than its neighbors:

- **Healthy fear** — threat is real, magnitude is proportionate, and there's something you can *do*. It resolves when the threat passes or the action works.
- **Phobia** — same machinery, **miscalibrated input.** The projection fires at content that isn't actually dangerous-at-that-magnitude, often a developmental imprint never updated to present life. The form is identical to real fear; only the calibration is off.
- **Panic** — the teaching case. Maximal arousal, *no articulable target.* In the architecture's terms it's **fully conscious but source-opaque** (the experience is fully present, A = 1; the "why" pathway φ is undefined). The paper is emphatic: panic is *"fully conscious, not almost unconscious"* — not a half-emerged subconscious thing, just an intense state whose source you can't reach.
- **Fear-for-the-beloved** — the same projection aimed at *X* rather than at yourself. And because losing certain extensions damages the self *more* than losing your own body does, this can be **more terrifying than fear for yourself.** A parent who would trade their life for their child's is making an architecturally accurate calculation about which loss damages the larger self more.

> **In plain terms —** fear is your self time-traveling to a moment where you get hurt, recoiling, and trying to head it off. It's "fear" specifically when you can name the danger and there's a move to make. Strip out the nameable target and you get panic; aim it at someone you love and it can be worse than any danger to yourself.

### Anger — a *violation of your frame*, met by pushing-away

**Anger is Mode A in response to a current frame-violation.** Here the operation isn't on an extension or a future — it's at the *boundary*. PtCns (the coherence-keeper) registers that some content — an act, an event, a state of affairs — *violates your frame*, something you cannot integrate as part of a coherent world. Mode A then fires to **differentiate against the violator and restore coherence.**

Healthy anger has somewhere to go: it resolves when (a) the violation is addressed, (b) it can't be addressed but you've done what was available and it de-escalates, (c) you realize you misread and there was no violation, or (d) your own frame revises to absorb what it had flagged — *sometimes you were the problem, not the encountered thing.* The pathologies are all variations on the push-away misfiring:

- **Helpless rage** — Mode A fires but *no action-pathway exists* (rage at the dead, at history, at structural injustice you can't personally fix). It's the boundary-cluster twin of dread: the operator running with nowhere to discharge. The paper notes its treatment resembles *grief-work*, not ordinary anger-resolution.
- **Displaced anger** — Mode A aimed at a *substitute* because the real violator is absent, unrecognized, or unsafe to confront. The operator is firing at a non-actual source.
- **Chronic resentment** — anger that never resolved and *settled in* as a background feature of the self.
- **Rage as PtCns hyperactivity** — the coherence-keeper miscalibrated so it sees violations *everywhere*; the paranoid configuration.

> **In plain terms —** anger is your self hitting something it *cannot accept* and shoving it back to stay whole. Healthy anger has a target and an exit. Helpless rage is the same shove with no wall to push against — which is why it's so exhausting, and why it has to be grieved rather than resolved.

### Joy-in-the-beloved — the extension *flourishing*

**Joy in the beloved's flourishing is the self in expansive state because an extension is.** Take love's configuration again and let X *thrive*. Because X is part of you, X flourishing *is you flourishing* — direct reading, not metaphor.

This is why a parent's joy in a child's success can *exceed* joy in their own: the child-as-extension may be *more* constitutive of who they are than parts of their own current life. Both are expansions of the same self; the child's success just lands more centrally given how that particular self is configured. The same structure explains:

- **Pride-in-beloved vs. pride-in-self** — same self expanding, different *part* of it as the source (a teacher's pride in a student, a coach's pride in a team are connection-cluster, not self-cluster).
- **Compersion** — joy in a beloved's joy *with someone else.* It's the exact inverse of possessive jealousy: instead of reading the extension as zero-sum, you read its flourishing-with-others as flourishing *as such.* The paper notes it's architecturally consistent but empirically uncommon, because it *requires high confidence in the invariance condition* — trust that the constitutive bond won't be displaced.

> **In plain terms —** when someone you love does well and you feel it *in your own chest* as if you'd done it — that's not you being generous, it's the architecture being literal. They're part of you, so their good is your good. Compersion is the rarer, more trusting version: feeling their joy as yours even when it comes from somewhere you're not.

### The schema underneath all four

Each derivation above is an instance of a fixed six-step recipe the paper applies to *every* affect: (1) which **region** of the self is affected; (2) the **dominant relation** — extension, reflexive evaluation, projected threat, boundary, or substrate; (3) which **operators** are active (Mode A/B/C, PtCns, PCE, focus); (4) the **κ/φ/ρ/Π/A signature** — contact, articulable-source, identity-correlation, priority-position, and how *available* it is to consciousness; (5) the **healthy constraint** that keeps it functional; (6) the **pathology mode** when that constraint fails (failure, hypertrophy, collapse, or misdirection).

> **In plain terms —** every emotion in the paper gets filled into the same blank form: *what part of me, doing what, with which tools, looking like what, kept healthy by what, and breaking how?* That shared form is why grief, fear, anger, and joy can be told apart precisely instead of just named.

## The five clusters

Real affect is then organized into five families, treated as overlapping *projections* (an
actual feeling is usually a compound — more than one of these operators running over the same region at once):

- **Connection** (extension affects) — **grief** (loss of a constitutive extension; "the
  shaking is literal" as the self reorganizes around the hole), fear-for-the-beloved,
  devotion, **jealousy** (threat to an extension), joy in another's flourishing. Plus the
  **dissolution** pathologies — obsession, codependency, cultic merger — where Mode C runs
  past the point the frame can survive: *not deeper love, but loss of the self that loved.*
- **Self** (frame affects) — the standout structural contribution. **Guilt, embarrassment,
  shame, and self-loathing are the same operation at different scopes** of reflexive
  self-evaluation: an *act* (guilt) → a *presentation* (embarrassment) → an *identity-aspect*
  (shame) → the *whole self* (self-loathing). Globalizing up that ladder is structurally
  easier than climbing back down — which is exactly why toxic shame is so hard to treat.
  Pride is the expansive (positive-valence) version. Shame also runs through a **witness**
  construct (actual, imagined, internalized, or retrospective), explaining shame inherited
  from standards you've consciously rejected.
- **Threat** (preservation affects) — **fear** (focused projected damage with an action
  available) vs. **anxiety** (diffuse projection across many futures — no single target, a *background tone* that exceeds anything you can name) vs. **dread** (likely
  damage with the action-space *foreclosed*); **hope** and **despair** as the presence or
  absence of a viable projected path. *Learned helplessness* is reclassified: not an emotion
  but a damaged *projection apparatus* — the self perceives the world as having no paths, so
  belief alone can't fix it; only scaffolded successful action rebuilds the tool.
- **Boundary** (encounter affects) — **anger** (a frame-violation, met by Mode A to restore
  coherence), **disgust** (content too intolerable to integrate, expelled — and *physical
  and moral disgust are the same form*, whose ugliest failure mode is disgust aimed at
  *persons*, i.e. dehumanization), **awe** (encounter with something too vast for the
  current frame), boredom, and **hate** (sustained Mode A where *every* resolution pathway
  has failed — pathological by construction).
- **Embodied** (somatic affects) — pain, pleasure, trauma-cue activation, mood. Organized by
  *pathway* rather than by relation, so this cluster sits crosswise to the other four.

> **In plain terms —** these five are like five directions an emotion can point: *toward someone you've taken in, back at yourself, ahead at a danger, out against an intruder, or up from the body.* Most real feelings point a few ways at once — grief is shot through with anger, fear braids into love — which is why the paper treats them as overlapping projections, not tidy boxes.

## Pathology as structure, not deficit

A throughline: severe states are **apparatus-level failures**, not just strong feelings. The
paper names a set — dissolution (frame), severe depression (the [cogito/thought partition
collapse](profile-shape-disturbances.md)), learned helplessness (projection apparatus),
chronic boredom (engagement-finding), anhedonia (pleasure-response) — each with distinct
damaged equipment and distinct recovery dynamics. **Panic** is the recurring teaching case:
maximal intensity, near-zero information, fully conscious but *source-opaque* — "fully
conscious, not almost unconscious."

> **In plain terms —** the worst states aren't ordinary emotions cranked to maximum; they're *broken instruments*. Learned helplessness isn't extreme sadness, it's a *projection tool that no longer finds any path* — which is why pep talks don't touch it and only walking someone through actual small successes rebuilds the tool. Naming *which piece of equipment* is broken is the difference between "feel better" and a repair plan.

## Status and stance

Complete, with **14 discriminating predictions** and a careful set of **translations** to
basic-emotion theory, appraisal theory, constructionism, affective neuroscience,
phenomenology, predictive coding, and attachment theory — "in dialogue with each," not
replacing them. The author is explicit that it is a **methodological exemplar, not an
exhaustive catalogue**, and that some of its evidence is her own first-person access to
depression, trauma activation, and suicidality — used as load-bearing data rather than
bracketed.
