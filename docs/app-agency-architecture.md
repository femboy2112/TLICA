## What it builds

Where *Free Will* defines free choice, *Agency Architecture* treats free choice as the
**canonical case** and derives *everything else about agency* as a structural variation of
it. Agency stops being a yes/no property and becomes an **I-relative profile** across several
dissociable axes: live-option construction, evaluative engagement, operator selection,
substrate coupling, [Mode B](modes-of-development.md) availability, ownership, and
[identity-correlation](three-coordinates.md). The will is handled strictly as a *function*,
not a faculty or a thing — there is willing, there are will-acts, but no "will-substance."

> **In plain terms —** the paper's first move is to refuse the question "do you have free will,
> yes or no?" Instead it asks "*what shape* does your agency have, right now, in this act?" —
> the way a doctor asks not "are you healthy?" but "what's your blood pressure, your sleep, your
> liver?" Agency is a profile of readings, not a single switch. And "the will" isn't a thing
> inside you that does the choosing; it's just the *name for the choosing itself* — like how
> "running" isn't an engine in your legs, it's what your legs are doing.

## The orders of willing

The foundation's three [imprinting pathways](substrate-focus-and-imprinting.md) describe how
the world reaches *in*; this paper turns them around to describe willing reaching *out*:

- **First-order** — directing focus and attention. (Choosing to look at the door instead of
  the screen.)
- **Second-order** — directing thought and deliberation. (Deciding to actually *think through*
  the budget instead of letting your mind wander.)
- **Third-order** — directing action through the body. (Reaching out and picking up the cup.)
- **Constructed orders (4+)** — willing through *other people and structures*: asking,
  coordinating a team, acting through an institution. (Getting something done by emailing a
  colleague, or by a company you started, rather than with your own hands.)

It also names three **strategies** of willing: **direct** (push toward the goal),
**configurational** (arrange your future environment so the habit forms itself), and
**slingshot** (time a small precise act to ride an existing gradient) — with explicit nods to
phronesis, kairos, and wu-wei. The hard constraint over all of it: *you cannot will what your
[toolkit](access-to-intrinsic-structure.md) cannot construct as a live option.*

> **In plain terms —** the three strategies are three honest ways to actually get yourself to
> do something. **Direct** is willpower: shove. **Configurational** is the trick of setting up
> your kitchen so the cookies aren't there to fight at midnight — you bend the *future you* by
> arranging the room now. **Slingshot** is waiting for the one right moment and giving a tiny
> nudge that the situation then amplifies for you — sending the text the instant the mood is
> right. The three foreign names are older idioms for this same family of moves, not
> decoration: *phronesis* is practical wisdom — knowing when to push, when to arrange, and when
> to wait; *kairos* is the ripe instant as against mere clock-time, the timing a slingshot
> lives or dies by; and *wu-wei* is effortless action that goes *with* the grain instead of
> against it. The paper is careful that these traditions track the *same* pattern in their own
> idioms — it doesn't pin each name to a single strategy. And the iron rule: you can only
> *choose* among options you can actually picture as real and reachable. You can't will
> yourself to a door you can't even see.

## The taxonomy of variation

The center of the paper is a **seventeen-family taxonomy** of agency configurations, each
read as a specific modification of the six conditions. They are organized by *family* (a
recognizable cluster like "akrasia" or "compulsion"), not condition-by-condition, because
each family has its own distinctive structural fingerprint that only shows up when you look at
the whole pattern at once:

- **akrasia** (the bundle selects against its own evaluation — you do the thing you'd already
  judged you shouldn't),
- **compulsion / addiction** (substrate *capture* — alternatives aren't live from inside),
- **ADHD-like configurations** (an implementation gap at the first two orders),
- **learned helplessness** (the projection toolkit has collapsed),
- **depression / anxiety contraction**, **dissociation / alienation** (ownership fails),
- **apparent choice / confabulation**, **manipulation**,
- **habit** (chosen vs. automatic vs. compulsive vs. skilled),
- **flow and mastery** (which *look* automatic from outside but keep ownership and clean
  sourcing intact),
- and **compounds** — because the clinically and philosophically interesting cases are almost
  always combinations.

The paper deliberately *opens* with the mild cases (thin and reduced agency) before the
pathologies, so the taxonomy doesn't read as a catalogue of dysfunctions. **Thin agency** is a
real choice made with low evaluative depth — picking between two equally-nice drinks, the
arbitrary button-press. It is not an illusion; it's just choosing where not much of *you* was
weighing in. **Reduced agency** is ordinary contraction — choosing while tired, rushed, mildly
distracted. Neither is a disorder; both are normal weather in a healthy agent.

> **In plain terms —** most of these "families" are things you've lived. Akrasia is
> reaching for the cigarette *while agreeing with yourself that you shouldn't* — the choosing
> part overrules the judging part. Compulsion is worse: the other options stop even *feeling*
> available from the inside. Dissociation is when the hand moves and it somehow doesn't feel
> like *yours*. Flow looks, from outside, like you're on autopilot — but inside, it's still
> fully you, just without the friction. The paper's gentleness is the point: choosing a soda
> without much thought is not a failure of free will; it's just thin agency, and that's fine.

### How each family is "read": the ten-axis diagnostic schema

Every variation is read against the **same ten-axis diagnostic schema** — a fixed checklist of
diagnostic questions. You don't have to score a case on all ten; most families are sharply
specified by just a few. The point is that any agency configuration has a *structurally
specifiable signature*. The axes ask:

1. **Live-option status** — written **𝒪 (the option-set, "𝒪 sub m,t;d")**, plainly *the menu
   of things you could actually pick right now*. Is it intact, contracted, poisoned, fuzzy, or
   absent?
2. **Condition profile** — which of the six free-choice conditions hold, are thin, fail, or are
   bypassed?
3. **Decision-event status** — was there an actual moment of committing? Real, minimal,
   apparent, or degraded?
4. **Order profile** — which orders of willing (1–3, and constructed 4+) are active, impaired,
   or absent?
5. **Effort profile** — what kind of *resistance* dominates, and did effort get spent, get
   exhausted, or never form?
6. **Mode B availability** — which agency-structures can you reflectively *take as an object*
   and look at, and which you can't.
7. **Source-status** — is the option-space source-clean, scaffolded, manipulated, poisoned,
   osmotically shaped, or coerced? (Where did these options *come from*, and is that source
   clean?)
8. **Ownership-status** — does the act register as *mine*, or as alien, dissociated, merely
   narrated, conflicted, imposed?
9. **Developmental window** — was your equipment still forming when this got laid down? (This
   one is a *responsibility overlay*, not a variation-classifier — see below.)
10. **Cascade profile** — the downstream ripples: direct, indirect, anticipated, unmanaged,
    repairable?

> **In plain terms —** think of these ten as the questions a careful friend would ask before
> deciding how much "you" was really behind something: *Did you actually have other options?
> Did you stop and weigh it? Did you commit, or just drift? Was the choice run through your
> body's pull or your attention or other people? How hard was it? Could you even step back and
> see yourself doing it? Where did the options come from — were they planted? Did it feel like
> yours? Were you grown-up and equipped when this took root? And what mess did it leave
> downstream?* Ten questions, and most cases only need three or four of them to come into
> focus.

The variation axis itself has four **sub-axes** that pin down a family quickly: *which
condition fails and at what order*; *which order(s) the act runs through*; *where substrate
coupling fails* (preemption, bypass, or capture); and *how clean the source is* (clean,
acutely manipulated, scaffold-captured, or coercively constructed). Read this way, the
families separate cleanly. **Akrasia** = condition-3 same-order failure (your evaluation and
your selection mismatch) with *no* substrate problem and *no* source problem — the act is
still fully yours. **Compulsion** = condition-4 *capture*. **Manipulation** = all six
conditions met inside you, but the menu itself was poisoned by someone else. **Alien-hand** =
condition-6 ownership failure. Same vocabulary, very different signatures.

> **In plain terms —** the cleanest way to tell two confusingly-similar cases apart is to ask
> *where* it breaks. Akrasia and compulsion can both feel like "I did the thing I didn't want
> to do" — but in akrasia the other options were still real to you and you simply chose against
> your own better judgment (it's *yours*), while in compulsion the alternatives stopped being
> live at all (the substrate hijacked the wheel). Manipulation is the sneaky one: everything
> inside you worked perfectly — you weighed it, you owned it, you chose freely — but somebody
> rigged the menu you were choosing *from*.

A subtle, useful result: **Mode B is one capacity but its objects are many, and its
availability is asymmetric** across orders — which is why "reflective access" can be present
for one kind of content and absent for another in the same person.

> **In plain terms —** *Mode B* is your ability to step back and look at your own workings, the
> way you'd inspect a thought instead of just having it. The surprise is that this isn't
> all-or-nothing. You can have crystal-clear insight into *one* part of yourself and a total
> blind spot for another, at the same time. A compulsive person can fully see and object to the
> act ("I know this is bad, I hate that I'm doing it") while having *no* reflective handle on
> the part that's actually pulling — objection-capacity without alteration-capacity. Seeing it
> and being able to change it are two different doors.

## Responsibility, done carefully

Agency without a theory of responsibility would be incomplete, so the paper builds one — and
insists it is **graded and profile-indexed, never zero-sum**. The triad from the companion —
**ownership / responsibility / accountability** — is kept strictly apart. Collapsing them is,
the paper argues, one of the most common errors in the philosophical literature, so it nails
each one down:

- **Ownership** — a *condition-6 relation*: is the act structurally integrated as *mine*?
  (Does it land as something I did?) This is **not** the same as it being clean, free, good, or
  legal.
- **Responsibility** — an *attribution relation*: am I structurally identifiable as the
  relevant agent-source of this act, *given* its ownership, source, developmental window, Mode
  B availability, foreseeability, and what repair options were live? This is **not** the same as
  freedom alone, or deserved blame.
- **Accountability** — an *answerability relation*: am I structurally addressable for
  explanation, repair, or response? This is **not** the same as punishment-deservingness or
  moral desert.

The three usually move together (a free, owned, clean act supports all three) but none reduces
to the others — and the architecture's sharpest cases live exactly in the *dissociations*,
where they come apart:

| Case | Ownership | Responsibility | Accountability |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Manipulated wholeheartedness** (you fully meant it, but your options were poisoned) | High | Reduced and qualified | Shared — you *and* the manipulator are addressable |
| **Alienated action** (dissociation — the act didn't feel yours) | Low | Reduced for the act; may relocate to prior formation or repair | May depend on supportive context |
| **Compulsion with intact objection** (you saw it, hated it, couldn't stop in the moment) | Conflicted | Reduced for the capture-moment; preserved for prior avoidance / treatment-seeking | Repair-accountability typically retained |
| **Source-clean owned mistake** | High | High | High |

> **In plain terms —** these three words usually get mashed into one, and the paper's whole
> discipline is to keep them apart. *Ownership* = "that was me." *Responsibility* = "and I'm the
> one the act traces back to, weighed against everything that was true of my situation."
> *Accountability* = "and so I'm the right person to explain it, or to help fix it." You can
> have one without the others. The manipulated true-believer fully *owns* what they did, yet
> bears *less* responsibility because the deck was stacked — and the person who stacked it
> becomes accountable too. The addict who hates the act in the moment doesn't get "off the
> hook"; their responsibility just relocates — backward to the choices that set it up, forward
> to the repair.

### The attribution profile

Every responsibility-judgment is read against one schema: the **attribution profile**, written

> **AP (the attribution profile, "AP sub m,t;a")** = ⟨V, O, S, C, B, D, K, M, R, T⟩

— a ten-component bundle for a given act. **V** is the variation-family (which of the 17, and
if compound, how they combine). **O** is ownership-status. **S** is source-status. **C** is the
condition-profile. **B** is Mode B availability. **D** is the developmental window. **K** is the
cascade profile. **M** is which mitigation/repair options were live. **R** is the role/order
profile. **T** is the temporal locus — was this about the *prior formation*, the *moment of
action*, the *downstream repair*, or *future prevention*?

The load-bearing commitment: *the joint profile is the attribution.* No single axis settles
responsibility. The same variation-family with different overlays gives different verdicts; the
same overlays with a different family give different verdicts. Responsibility is *computed from
the whole configuration*, never read off one reading.

> **In plain terms —** picture a ten-dial mixing board, and the final responsibility verdict is
> the whole board, not any one fader. Two people can both be "in addiction" (same V) but land in
> very different places once you set the other dials — one is a grown adult with live treatment
> options, the other a trauma-shaped person during the formative years with almost no live
> alternatives. Same family, different profile, different answer. The letters are just shorthand
> for "which mess is it, was it yours, where'd the options come from, were you equipped, what
> could you still do about it, and at what point in time are we even asking?"

The paper also insists the **objects** of attribution be split: you can be responsible for the
*decision* but not the *outcome*, for the *action* but not the *cascade*. Decision, trying,
action, outcome, cascade, and **profile-formation** each get their own profile. Conflating them
— "the result was bad, therefore the decision was bad" — is exactly the error the apparatus is
engineered to prevent.

> **In plain terms —** "it turned out badly" is not the same accusation as "you decided badly."
> You can make a clean, careful decision and have luck or someone else wreck the outcome — and
> you're answerable for the deciding, not the dice. The architecture forces you to say *which*
> thing you're holding the person to.

Two disciplines recur. First, *"reduction is not absolution"*: diminished agency lowers
attribution without erasing accountability — and crucially, the paper refuses *both* lazy
defaults, neither "they're sick, so off the hook" nor "they're responsible regardless." Each
case earns its own profile. Second, a **developmental-window overlay** conditions attribution
when the agent's equipment was still forming. The window has five settings:

| Window | What it means | Effect on attribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-agential** | The pattern was imprinted *before* you had the toolkit at all | Strong reduction for the formation; source may attach to caretakers / environment |
| **Immature** | Partial toolkit, unstable option-construction | Partial, domain-specific; the adult standard doesn't yet apply |
| **Mature** | Toolkit and Mode B normally available | The default, full standard — the baseline the others attenuate from |
| **Scaffold-captured** | Mature capacities running *inside* an externally-built option-space (cult, total institution) | Split: real attribution for within-scaffold acts, compromised source for the space itself — the hardest cell |
| **Trauma-shaped** | Mature toolkit, but option-construction and projection shaped by prior threat | Qualified by what options *remain* live; reduction is not absolution |

> **In plain terms —** this is the difference between "you chose this as a capable adult" and
> "this got wired into you as a kid, before you had any say." A habit drilled into you at age
> six and the same habit you cultivated at thirty are the *same structural habit* — but you're
> answerable for them very differently. The brutal middle case is the cult member: inside their
> world everything works — they choose, they mean it, they reflect — but the *world itself* was
> built around them to keep the exits invisible. The paper won't pretend they chose nothing, and
> won't pretend they chose freely.

There's even a notion of *responsibility for becoming* — for the profile you helped shape over
time. Configurational willing (arranging your future self) propagates *forward*: if you
deliberately, cleanly, with eyes open trained yourself into a configuration, you own that
configuration's later operation. The person who built the discipline owns the discipline; the
person who, as a capable adult, cultivated the bad loop carries some of it forward — to the
extent the original arranging was source-clean, live, and within what they could have foreseen.

> **From the inside —** some of who you are now, you *built* — by what you practiced, who you
> spent time around, the rooms you put yourself in. The paper's claim is that this is real and
> trackable: you can be partly answerable not just for what you did today but for *making
> yourself into the kind of person who would do it* — but only to the degree that, back then,
> you could see where it led and the building was genuinely your own.

## Reading the experiments

A whole section is devoted to the **empirical interface**, and its core move is a discipline:
lab paradigms test *narrow* objects, not "free will." It separates the timing markers that
get conflated — preparation, selection, report, movement, outcome, recognition are all
*different moments* — and concludes that, e.g., Libet measures thin *free selection*, the
readiness potential is *preparation, not preemption*, and Wegner's results sort into five
distinct configurations rather than one global "agency is illusory." It promotes its
operational candidates into **eighteen formal predictions**, with the ownership/sourcehood
dissociation under manipulation flagged as the flagship discriminator.

> **In plain terms —** the famous "neuroscience disproved free will" experiments mostly tested
> the *thinnest* possible thing — flicking a finger whenever — and then announced a verdict on
> *all of choosing*. The paper's reply is to slow the film down: getting *ready* to move,
> *committing* to move, *reporting* that you committed, the *movement*, the *result*, and
> *recognizing* it afterward are six different frames, and the experiments smear them together.
> A brain signal that shows up before you report deciding is your system warming up — not your
> brain secretly deciding *for* you.

## What it leaves open

Extensively catalogued (and honestly so): how to *weight* the components of an attribution
profile; where the floor of "no accountability at all" sits; operational tests for
source-status and Mode B (named the highest-priority bottleneck); modeling group agency
*without* a group-mind; and clinical translation, which it explicitly marks as **not yet
validated and not treatment advice**. On the group question, the paper holds a firm line:
collective responsibility is a *distributed pattern* of individual, perspective-anchored
attributions plus constructed-order scaffold effects — never a free-standing "group mind."
Some items are flagged as *principled limits* (e.g. neural implementation of the
operator-bundle — welcome to map, but reducing the architecture to it would be a failure)
rather than to-do items.

> **In plain terms —** the paper is candid about what it can't yet do. It can tell you the
> *shape* of a responsibility-judgment but not the exact *weights* — how much each dial counts.
> It can describe a group acting badly as many individual people, in their roles, plus the
> structures that bent their options — but it refuses to invent a "the company decided" mind
> floating above the actual humans. And it keeps repeating: this is a map of structure, not a
> diagnosis and not advice.

Its closing line mirrors the companion's: the taxonomy *"does not deliver verdicts on
agency-or-not. It makes the analysis structurally navigable."*

> **In plain terms —** it won't hand you a yes-or-no on whether someone "really" chose, or
> "really" is to blame. What it gives you is a clean way to *take the case apart* — to see
> exactly which piece worked, which broke, and where the answerability actually sits.
