## The move: stop asking the one big question

The paper doesn't argue for or against free will. It argues that the *question* is the
problem: "was that an exercise of free will?" bundles a dozen structurally distinct issues
into a single verdict, which is why the debate feels permanently stuck. Its solution is to
**replace the one contested property with a structural definition** — and then track the
components separately, without zero-sum collapse.

The reason the old question jams is that a single word ("free") is being asked to carry
several different jobs at once: *were there real alternatives?*, *did I weigh anything?*,
*did the choice come from me or from my body's machinery?*, *could I have caught myself
doing it if I looked?*, *is the thing I did really mine?* These can come apart. A heroin
craving and a careful career decision are both, loosely, "things I did" — but they fail and
pass these sub-questions in completely different places. The architecture's wager is that
once you split the one verdict into its parts, the parts each become answerable.

> **In plain terms —** Instead of asking the unanswerable "do humans have free will, yes or
> no?", this page asks a set of smaller, concrete questions about the *shape* of a particular
> act. Most of the philosophical deadlock comes from cramming all of those into one word.

## Free choice as six conditions

"Free choice proper" is defined as a configuration meeting **six interdependent conditions**.
They are not a scoring checklist where you tally points; they are six joints of *one*
mechanism, and they hold each other up — knock one out and the failure propagates through the
rest. Here is each condition unfolded, with the everyday version next to it:

1. **A contrastive live-option space** — your mental toolkit (the "verification-toolkit": the
   developed capacity that constructs, evaluates, and lets you inhabit possible courses)
   actually builds **two or more real alternatives**, *including* the option of refusing,
   waiting, or doing nothing. "Live" doesn't mean fully scripted — the cook who decides "pasta
   tonight" without planning every knife-stroke still has a live option, because they have a
   *trained expectation* that the details will fill in as they go. What makes that expectation
   trustworthy is real prior skill in the domain, not wishful "I'll figure it out."
2. **Evaluative engagement** — you actually bring your values to bear on the options. In the
   theory's terms, this is where the [prerogatives](the-two-prerogatives.md) **PtCns**
   (your drive to stay *consistent* — to keep making sense as one self) and **PCE** (your
   drive toward *continued existence* — keeping yourself going), the
   **[preservation-ranking Π](the-two-prerogatives.md)** (in plain terms, your felt
   priority-order over what matters most to protect), and a sense of what's *relevant to the
   frame* all get applied to the choices. This can be quick and wordless in a practiced area —
   a chess master "feels" a move is wrong without a speech — but something has to be weighing.
3. **Operator-bundle selection** — the choice comes out of **your own** coordinated machinery
   for deciding (the "operator-bundle"), not from outside you and not from your body firing
   on its own.
4. **Substrate coupling without preemption, bypass, or capture** — your **substrate** (your
   body-and-brain, the physical host the choice runs on) *carries* the choice but doesn't
   *make* it for you. Three distinct ways it can hijack the act: **preemption** (the body acts
   before you've selected — a reflex), **bypass** (the act slips out through a body-pathway
   without ever entering your live-option weighing — some compulsions), and **capture** (one
   pull is so dominant that the alternatives stop feeling *live* from the inside — addiction).
5. **Non-confabulatory [Mode B](modes-of-development.md) availability** — **Mode B** is your
   capacity to turn around and take your own choosing as an *object* of reflection: to look at
   what you're doing as you do it. The condition is only that this access *be available if you
   look* — you don't have to actually be reflecting at the moment, and a later story you tell
   about it doesn't count if the story is made-up rather than tracking what really happened.
6. **Ownership-integration** — the chosen option gets taken up as *yours*: endorsed and woven
   into your sense of who you are, rather than felt as alien, merely undergone, inserted from
   outside, or produced by a split-off part of you.

> **In plain terms —** A choice is fully "free choice" when: real alternatives were on the
> table; you weighed them against what you care about; the decision came from you; your body
> carried it without yanking the wheel; you *could have* watched yourself do it; and the
> result feels like *yours*. Drop any one of those and you get a specific, recognizable kind
> of unfree act rather than a vague "less free."

> **From the inside —** It feels like: "I saw I could go either way, I cared which way it
> went, I picked, my hands did it because I told them to, I knew what I was doing, and yes —
> that was *me*."

### The three tiers — not a value ladder

The six conditions sit inside a wider three-tier picture, and the paper is emphatic that this
is **not** a ranking of better-vs-worse choices, just three structurally different shapes:

- **Free selection** (thin) — the toolkit builds options, you pick, your body does it, you own
  it, but the *evaluating* is minimal or absent. Flipping a coin between two equally good
  desserts; the spontaneous left-or-right button-press in a Libet experiment; routine skilled
  motions. This is *thin*-free, not *un*-free.
- **Free choice proper** — all six conditions hold. The deliberated, weighed, owned decision
  the philosophy literature usually has in mind.
- **Wholehearted free choice** (deep) — all six, *plus* the choice is in deep **Π-alignment**:
  it expresses your most central, settled commitments, not just something you'll accept as
  yours. The act belongs to your structural *center*, not merely to your action-history.

A key diagnostic falls straight out of this: the famous **Libet experiment** (where brain
activity precedes the conscious decision to press a button) tests *free selection* — subjects
are told to act spontaneously, with no deliberation invited. Taking it to refute free choice
*in general* is a tier-confusion: it disproves something nobody needed to defend.

> **In plain terms —** Picking a flavor on a whim, making a careful call, and acting from the
> deepest grain of who you are are three *different things*, not three rungs of one ladder.
> Lots of famous "free will is an illusion" arguments quietly test the thinnest one and then
> announce a verdict on the deepest.

## When conditions fail: the named modifications

This is where the framework earns its keep. Each recognizable failure of agency is a
**specific** condition breaking in a **specific** way — not a generic loss of freedom. The
same six joints, broken at different points, give you a clean structural map.

### Akrasia (weakness of will) — Condition 3, same-order split

**Akrasia** is the classic "I knew better and did it anyway": you eat the cake you'd just
decided not to, you keep scrolling when you meant to sleep. Structurally, the theory locates
it precisely: it's a **Condition 3** failure where evaluation and selection come apart *at the
same level*. Your evaluative engagement (Condition 2) produces one verdict — "don't" — and the
operator-bundle nonetheless selects the other thing. The bundle selects **against its own
evaluation**. Crucially, this is *not* your body acting on its own (that would be bypass or
preemption); the selection still ran through you. That's exactly why akrasia feels like a
personal failing rather than an accident.

> **In plain terms —** Akrasia isn't your hand moving by itself. You *chose* the thing you'd
> already judged you shouldn't — the deciding-part overrode the weighing-part, and both were
> you. That internal contradiction is what gives it its particular sting of self-betrayal.

### Compulsion — Condition 4 (bypass) or Condition 3

**Compulsion** — the hand-washing you can't not do, the checking, the tic — comes in two
structurally distinct forms in the theory, and that distinction matters. In its **bypass**
form, the act slips out through a substrate pathway **without ever entering live-option
selection at all** (a Condition 4 failure): there was no moment where alternatives were on the
table and you picked. In other forms it shades toward a Condition-3 problem where selection is
produced by the substrate before the operator-bundle properly engages. Either way, what's
missing isn't *willpower* — it's that the choosing-machinery was routed around.

> **In plain terms —** A compulsion doesn't feel like losing an argument with yourself (that's
> akrasia). It feels like the act happening *through* you before any argument could even
> start — the part of you that would have weighed it never got the call.

### Addiction — Condition 4 (capture)

**Addiction** is the theory's clean case of **capture**: a substrate-pull so dominant that
the alternatives **stop being live from the inside**. The toolkit can no longer construct
"don't use" as a genuinely inhabitable option at sufficient resolution to engage it — the
option-space itself has collapsed under the pull. This is why "just choose differently" misses
what's broken: Condition 1 (live alternatives) and Condition 4 (no capture) are *both*
failing. The other course isn't merely hard to pick; from inside, it has stopped being a real
course to pick *among*.

> **In plain terms —** Addiction isn't a fair fight you keep losing. The pull is strong enough
> that the alternative quietly stops appearing on the menu — you can't really weigh against an
> option your mind can no longer hold up as live. That's why it's a capture of the option-space,
> not a shortage of grit.

> **From the inside —** it doesn't feel like losing a fight you're having. It feels
> like the fight was never available — you go looking for the "just don't" option the
> way you'd look for a door that used to be in the wall, and it simply isn't there
> anymore.

### Manipulation — Condition 6 owned, but sourcehood compromised

Manipulation is the theory's sharpest tool, because it breaks the usual picture in a
surprising place. The manipulated agent can satisfy *all six internal conditions* — including
**Condition 6, ownership**: they fully endorse the act, it feels like theirs, it even fits
their values. What's wrong is **upstream**: the *option-space they were choosing among was
shaped from outside*. The paper's load-bearing distinction is that **owning a choice and where
the choice was sourced are dissociable**:

- You can **own** a decision completely from the inside while its **source** was compromised
  from the outside — that *is* manipulation.
- You can also **fail to own** a perfectly clean act — that's dissociation (alien-hand
  syndrome, dissociative action), where Condition 6 breaks while sourcehood was fine.

This is why manipulation is genuinely *hard*: there's no single lever to pull. And it's why
responsibility splits **between** manipulator and manipulated without being a zero-sum
quantity — the theory carries a triad, **ownership / responsibility / accountability**, to
keep three things apart that the literature keeps fusing. Responsibility is **not a conserved
quantity**: attributing it to the manipulator does *not* automatically subtract it from the
manipulated agent. Both can hold real, profile-indexed attributions at once. A
**developmental-window** overlay then conditions how much applies when the agent's evaluative
equipment was still being formed.

The paper rules out three tempting shortcuts: **naïve absolution** ("they were manipulated, so
they're not responsible at all" — this erases their real ownership), **naïve transfer** ("all
of it moves to the manipulator" — treats responsibility like a fixed amount of fluid), and
**naïve preservation** ("they chose it, so it's fully on them" — ignores the rigged
option-space). It also splits *acute* manipulation (a single gaslighting episode → concentrated
blame on one manipulator) from *distributed* manipulation (long propaganda, ideological
scaffolding → spread across many actors and conditioned by when in development it landed).

> **In plain terms —** The eerie thing about being manipulated is that it *feels* like a
> free, owned choice — because in the narrow sense it was. The damage is in the rigging of
> what you got to choose between. So the blame doesn't all move to the manipulator and it
> doesn't all stay with you; both of you carry your own share, and the shares don't have to
> add up to a fixed total.

> **From the inside —** nothing feels off in the moment. You weighed it, you meant it,
> it's unmistakably yours — the wrongness was never in how the choosing felt, it's in a
> menu you never even saw get rigged.

### Flow / skilled free action — a healthy modification, not a failure

Not every modification is a pathology. **Flow** — the absorbed expert performance, the
musician mid-passage, the practiced execution — is what the paper calls **skilled free
action**: free choice run *across coordinated orders* without explicit moment-to-moment
deliberation. The evaluating is real but **compressed and pre-compiled** (the expert's "feel"
for the wrong note), and **Mode B reflective access stays available** — the expert can pause
and reflect on what they were doing if they need to, which is exactly why it still counts.
Ownership stays intact throughout. Flow is *not* unfreedom or autopilot; it's free choice
exercised through highly developed cross-order coordination, where the deliberation has been
folded into trained competence rather than removed.

> **In plain terms —** In flow you're not on autopilot and you're not deliberating either —
> you're choosing fast and fluently because the weighing has been baked into the skill. The
> tell that it's still *free* is that you could snap out and reflect on it any second; you
> just don't need to.

### The shared map

Notice the pattern: each named state is *one* condition breaking in *one* way.

| State | What breaks | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Akrasia** | Condition 3 | selection runs against its own evaluation, same level |
| **Compulsion** | Condition 4 (bypass) / 3 | act exits without entering live-option selection |
| **Addiction** | Condition 4 (capture) + 1 | pull so strong alternatives stop being live |
| **Manipulation** | Condition 6 *intact*, sourcehood compromised | owned act, externally-rigged option-space |
| **Flow / skilled action** | *no* failure | deliberation compressed; Mode B still available |

> **In plain terms —** These aren't five flavors of "kinda unfree." They're five different
> joints in the same mechanism, each giving way (or, for flow, *not* giving way) in its own
> recognizable way. That's the whole payoff of splitting the one big question into six.

## Where it lands in the old debate (a fifth position)

The paper deliberately refuses all four classic boxes:

- **Not libertarian** — it rejects the idea that freedom needs quantum indeterminism;
  "randomness is not sourcehood." A dice-roll in your neurons would give you none of the six
  conditions; an unpredictable choice isn't thereby a *yours* choice.
- **Not standard compatibilism** — it doesn't rescue freedom by *weakening* what freedom
  means.
- **Not hard determinist / illusionist** — it rejects "some reports are confabulated,
  therefore agency is an illusion." Some self-reports really are made-up (that's a Condition 5
  failure), but that's a *specific failure mode*, not a verdict on the whole.
- It is **implementation-neutral**: the six conditions can be satisfied whether the
  substrate is deterministic or stochastic. Free choice is "real as an inside-perspective
  architectural configuration, but it is not simple."

The only metaphysical thing the architecture *does* require is a **lawful structural frame** —
enough regularity for its objects (the coordinates **κ** = *contact*, your degree of
acquaintance with a content; **φ** = *truth-indistinguishability* (in this agency context also read with the operational
gloss *source-pathway / truth-verification access*), your grip on where a content came from and
whether it's real; **ρ** = *identity-correlation*, how much
of *you* is bound up in a thing; and **Π**, your priority-order) to even be defined. Randomness
is fine; total frame-level chaos is not — but that's an *applicability* condition, upstream of
all six, not a seventh condition of freedom.

It reconstructs the classic demands structurally: **"could have done otherwise"** becomes
*the option-space held alternatives, the bundle wasn't preempted, and the choice was owned*
— not a metaphysical rerun of the universe. The theory doesn't promise that, rewinding the
cosmos atom-for-atom, you'd have picked differently; it shows that, in every sense a real
agent can occupy from the inside, the other doors were open and yours.

> **In plain terms —** The theory takes a fifth seat at a table set for four. It won't tell
> you the universe could have gone differently — it says that's the wrong thing to want.
> What you actually need for freedom is that the doors were really there, the choice came
> from you, and you can own it. That you can have, determinism or not.

## Ownership vs. sourcehood — why manipulation is hard

The paper's sharpest tool: **owning a choice and where the choice was sourced are
dissociable.** You can fully own a decision from the inside while its source was compromised
from the outside (manipulation); you can also fail to own a perfectly clean act
(dissociation). This is why manipulation is genuinely difficult — and why it splits
responsibility *between* manipulator and manipulated without either being a zero-sum
quantity. The accompanying triad — **ownership / responsibility / accountability** — keeps
those three apart, and a **developmental-window** overlay conditions how much attribution
applies when the agent's evaluative equipment was still being formed.

The three relations the triad pries apart: **ownership** is whether the act integrated as
yours (Condition 6); **responsibility** is the attribution of *source* for the act, sensitive
to how clean the option-space was, how formed you were, and whether you had live repair
options; **accountability** is whether you're *addressable* for explanation or repair — which
can survive even where original-act responsibility is reduced (you may still owe repair for
downstream damage you didn't fully author).

> **In plain terms —** "It was mine," "it was my fault," and "I owe an answer for it" are
> three different claims that everyday language smears together. Keeping them apart is what
> lets the theory say sensible things about hard cases instead of an all-or-nothing verdict.

## What it leaves open

As a draft, it's candid about its edges. It separates **principled limits** (things the
architecture *can't* settle: ultimate sourcehood, why there's a lawful frame at all, whether
the structural inputs are *normatively enough* for moral responsibility) from **open work**
(formal weighting among the attribution components, operational tests for source-status and
Mode B). It engages eight other frameworks under a "**translate, don't refute**" discipline —
Frankfurt-style hierarchies, reasons-responsiveness, attributionism, hard incompatibilism,
libertarianism, illusionism/Wegner, predictive coding, and the phenomenology/wisdom
traditions — keeping what each gets right and marking exactly where it diverges.

> **In plain terms —** The paper is honest that it supplies the *structural inputs* to
> questions of blame and freedom, not the final moral verdict. It can tell you the shape of an
> act with precision; whether that shape is *enough* to deserve praise or blame depends on a
> moral theory it deliberately doesn't try to settle for you.

Its closing line: the account *"does not end the free-will debate by declaring victory. It
makes the debate structurally navigable."*
