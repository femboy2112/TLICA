## The move: stop asking the one big question

The paper doesn't argue for or against free will. It argues that the *question* is the
problem: "was that an exercise of free will?" bundles a dozen structurally distinct issues
into a single verdict, which is why the debate feels permanently stuck. Its solution is to
**replace the one contested property with a structural definition** — and then track the
components separately, without zero-sum collapse.

## Free choice as six conditions

"Free choice proper" is defined as a configuration meeting **six interdependent conditions**:

1. **A contrastive live-option space** — your toolkit actually constructs two or more real
   alternatives (including refusal).
2. **Evaluative engagement** — the [prerogatives](the-two-prerogatives.md), the
   [preservation-ranking Π](the-two-prerogatives.md), and frame-relevance are brought to bear.
3. **Operator-bundle selection** — the choice emerges from your own operators, not from
   outside.
4. **Substrate coupling without preemption, bypass, or capture** — your substrate carries
   the choice but doesn't *make* it for you (reflex = preemption; some compulsions = bypass;
   addiction = capture).
5. **Non-confabulatory [Mode B](modes-of-development.md) availability** — the process is
   reflectively *accessible if you look*, even if you don't.
6. **Ownership-integration** — the option is endorsed and integrated as *yours*, not alien
   or inserted.

These come in **three tiers**, explicitly *not* a value ranking: *free selection* (thin —
flipping a coin between two equal desserts, the Libet button-press), *free choice proper*
(all six), and *wholehearted free choice* (all six plus deep alignment with your most
central commitments). A key diagnostic claim: the famous Libet experiment tests *free
selection* and is wrongly taken to refute free choice in general.

## Where it lands in the old debate (a fifth position)

The paper deliberately refuses all four classic boxes:

- **Not libertarian** — it rejects the idea that freedom needs quantum indeterminism;
  "randomness is not sourcehood."
- **Not standard compatibilism** — it doesn't rescue freedom by *weakening* what freedom
  means.
- **Not hard determinist / illusionist** — it rejects "some reports are confabulated,
  therefore agency is an illusion."
- It is **implementation-neutral**: the six conditions can be satisfied whether the
  substrate is deterministic or stochastic. Free choice is "real as an inside-perspective
  architectural configuration, but it is not simple."

It reconstructs the classic demands structurally: **"could have done otherwise"** becomes
*the option-space held alternatives, the bundle wasn't preempted, and the choice was owned*
— not a metaphysical rerun of the universe.

## Ownership vs. sourcehood — why manipulation is hard

The paper's sharpest tool: **owning a choice and where the choice was sourced are
dissociable.** You can fully own a decision from the inside while its source was compromised
from the outside (manipulation); you can also fail to own a perfectly clean act
(dissociation). This is why manipulation is genuinely difficult — and why it splits
responsibility *between* manipulator and manipulated without either being a zero-sum
quantity. The accompanying triad — **ownership / responsibility / accountability** — keeps
those three apart, and a **developmental-window** overlay conditions how much attribution
applies when the agent's evaluative equipment was still being formed.

## What it leaves open

As a draft, it's candid about its edges. It separates **principled limits** (things the
architecture *can't* settle: ultimate sourcehood, why there's a lawful frame at all, whether
the structural inputs are *normatively enough* for moral responsibility) from **open work**
(formal weighting among the attribution components, operational tests for source-status and
Mode B). It engages eight other frameworks under a "**translate, don't refute**" discipline —
Frankfurt-style hierarchies, reasons-responsiveness, attributionism, hard incompatibilism,
libertarianism, illusionism/Wegner, predictive coding, and the phenomenology/wisdom
traditions — keeping what each gets right and marking exactly where it diverges.

Its closing line: the account *"does not end the free-will debate by declaring victory. It
makes the debate structurally navigable."*
