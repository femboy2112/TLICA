# This Ontology Is Really Moreish

## Underground Super Hans, Pavlov’s Veruca, and the Greedy Integral of the Present

**Abstract**

A peculiar failure mode appears when individually defensible intuitions combine: that a finite embedded agent cannot completely predict its own future; that present experience has a phenomenological actuality unavailable to merely projected futures; that a life worth living should remain self-congruent rather than being indefinitely sacrificed to abstractions; and that moral seriousness may require real self-sacrifice rather than automatically externalizing costs onto others. Taken together, these intuitions can generate locally compelling but globally destructive policies when scoped observations are promoted into universal governors.

This paper develops that architecture through three deliberately exaggerated figures. Underground Super Hans represents adversarial epistemic freedom: skepticism toward imposed plans, future narratives, respectable scripts, and claims to authority over one’s trajectory. Pavlov’s Veruca represents the immediately reinforced demand that worthwhile life be available now, not merely promised later. The Jesus-ideal represents sacrificial moral seriousness: the demand that one not casually privilege one’s own comfort over harms imposed on others. Each contains something I still regard as true. The failure occurs when a useful sensor is promoted to governor.

Their interaction sits inside what I call the Greedy Integral Problem: attempting to maximize the total meaningfulness of a life by maximizing each moment independently, while neglecting the state dynamics through which present choices alter the feasible futures from which later moments will be lived. A second failure appears in the way I learned such truths: an admired or apparently enlightened thinker supplied a candidate invariant, I phenomenologically probed it, and if I repeatedly felt and noticed the pattern I internalized it as another truth. The method was empirical in aspiration; the bug was coarse compilation. Local truth became global policy.

The resulting worldview is “moreish” in a precise phenomenological sense. It converts constraint into rebellion, uncertainty into license, helplessness into authorship, downstream consequences into fresh evidence for the very frame that helped produce them, and moral cost into something the self may volunteer to absorb. The corrective is therefore not conventional long-range planning, cynicism, or the abandonment of sacrifice. It is the construction of adaptive policies whose invariants survive uncertainty: preserve corrigibility, preserve future option value, avoid gratuitous irreversible commitments, keep affective telemetry inside the control loop, and refuse to confuse moral willingness to bear cost with assigning the self zero weight.

1. The child philosopher accidentally builds a controller

Suppose one begins with an apparently sober premise.

Reality is not completely knowable from inside itself.

A finite agent occupies a local, lossy position within a world whose state exceeds the agent’s available representation. Observation is itself interaction. Future states are not presently available as actuality but must be generated through projection, approximation, and extrapolation.

Nothing especially pathological follows from this.

Indeed, the epistemic lesson is useful:

> model ≠ world.

The trouble begins when this epistemic distinction quietly becomes a normative one:

> future models are uncertain

therefore

> future models deserve weak authority over present life.

And from there:

> **Do not sacrifice the actually lived present to speculative futures.**

This can sound almost ascetic in its seriousness.

It is also an excellent philosophy for accidentally ruining your credit score.

The architecture developed here is not simply “impulsivity.” It is more sophisticated and therefore more dangerous. It contains an explicit epistemology, a theory of agency, a suspicion of abstraction, and a positive conception of authentic life.

It needs characters.

2. Underground Super Hans

Dostoevsky’s Underground Man is trapped partly because reflection has ceased being a tool and become a habitat. Consciousness recursively attacks every attempted closure. Any sufficient reason can be reopened. Any externally supplied meaning can be contaminated by the fact that it was externally supplied.

Super Hans contributes a different flavor.

The Hans-like move is not merely:

«I reject this.»

It is:

«I have noticed the game itself is absurd, so I refuse to grant its categories full jurisdiction over me.»

This produces Underground Super Hans: the epistemic saboteur who lives in the gap between a model and the world.

He performs several legitimate functions.

He detects institutional bullshit.

He notices when projected narratives become mistaken for necessity.

He resists surrendering present agency to abstractions.

He distrusts scripts that demand enormous sacrifice today in exchange for poorly specified future rewards.

He preserves the possibility that the entire framing of the problem is wrong.

In control language, he is an extraordinarily useful adversarial auditor.

Unfortunately, teenage philosophy may accidentally promote him to governor.

Then skepticism ceases merely constraining plans and begins governing the temporal discounting function itself.

The future says:

«If you repeatedly do this, consequences will accumulate.»

Underground Super Hans replies:

«That sounds suspiciously like a model, mate.»

The observation is formally true.

Its use is catastrophic.

3. Pavlov’s Veruca

The second figure is less epistemological.

She wants it now.

Veruca Salt is useful here not as a literal psychological characterization but as an exaggerated representation of a reward function:

> $R_t \approx$ quality of presently available experience.

The point is not simple hedonism.

The deeper demand is:

«A life whose value exists only in projection is not presently a valuable life.»

Again, this contains truth.

A person cannot indefinitely justify an intolerable present by appealing to a hypothetical future paradise.

There are lives structured around endless postponement:

- happiness after graduation,
- freedom after promotion,
- rest after the next deadline,
- authenticity after financial security,
- art after everything practical has finally been handled.

The sequence may never terminate.

Pavlov’s Veruca therefore performs a genuine diagnostic role. She asks whether the system is receiving actual reward, rather than merely being trained to pursue indefinitely deferred symbols.

But once coupled to immediate reinforcement, the principle becomes:

> if action $a_t$ produces immediate resonance, repeat $a_t$.

And repeated local reward can condition behavior before its slower consequences become phenomenologically salient.

Hence Pavlov’s Veruca.

She is not merely demanding pleasure.

She is the part of the system learning:

> present resonance ⇒ good policy.

That inference is the second bug.

3.5. The enlightened-person compiler

There was another mechanism underneath all of this that I did not initially recognize as a mechanism because, from the inside, it looked like intellectual honesty.

My rough method for acquiring truths was:

> hear / see / read something an enlightened person noticed → probe it phenomenologically → if I feel and notice the pattern, internalize it as truth.

This was not simple deference to authority.

The admired thinker supplied a candidate.

I attempted replication.

If Jesus appeared to have noticed something about moral behavior and self-sacrifice, I looked for that structure in life.

If the Buddha appeared to have noticed that attachment and suffering were deeply entangled, I looked for that structure in life.

If I could repeatedly feel and notice the pattern, the observation entered the ontology.

There is something admirable in this method.

Do not merely inherit claims.

Test whether reality seems to contain the thing being described.

The bug is that phenomenological replication does not automatically identify the correct scope of the proposition.

> **I can repeatedly encounter a pattern ⇏ the broadest possible policy built from that pattern is valid.**

“Attachment contributes to suffering” can be true without “attachment should be globally minimized” being a good policy.

“Self-sacrifice can be morally exemplary” can be true without “the self should become the designated sink for moral cost” following from it.

“Models are corrigible” can be true without “future models deserve almost no authority” following from it.

The failure was not that every internalized truth was false.

The interpretation was too coarse.

Worse, the update law became asymmetric.

Phenomenological resonance could help a proposition enter:

> candidate truth + felt/noticed recurrence → internalization.

But once internalized, phenomenological suffering could fail to make it leave:

> pain while implementing the rule ⇏ the rule is false.

That logical observation is often correct.

Its use as a controller is catastrophic.

I had accidentally constructed an epistemic ratchet:

> **phenomenology may canonize the rule, but phenomenology cannot easily de-canonize it.**

The system could learn from lived resonance and then refuse to learn symmetrically from lived damage.

3.6. Jesus with root access

The third figure is stranger because I never needed to consider myself conventionally Christian for him to matter.

I was Christian-coded: baptized, exposed to Sunday school early, surrounded by more explicit Christianity through my grandparents, but not raised under a rigidly enforced doctrinal household. Later Hitchens and other skeptical influences mattered too.

None of that prevented the reported person Jesus from occupying a very specific place in my moral imagination.

Look at what this person allegedly did.

He treated moral behavior as expensive.

He repeatedly privileged the vulnerable.

He accepted suffering rather than simply pushing every cost outward.

He made self-sacrifice look like evidence of moral seriousness.

As a candidate invariant, this contains something I still hold dear.

The moral question

«Am I protecting myself by making somebody else pay the cost?»

is a very good sensor.

The bug appears when WWJD gets root access.

Combine the Golden Rule, uncertainty about God, a physics-shaped intuition that every action perturbs the environment, a relativity-coded refusal to grant my own frame magical privilege, and an idealization of sacrificial goodness.

The resulting controller can become:

> **drive the negative moral perturbations I produce toward zero.**

And because exact moral zero is neither observable nor obviously coherent, the self becomes the most available actuator.

If somebody must absorb a cost, I know where I can send the command.

Me.

This is not what follows from Jesus.

It is what follows when a scoped moral truth is compiled too coarsely.

The moral sensor says:

«Do not treat yourself as the only person who matters.»

The pathological governor says:

«Therefore treat yourself as though you barely matter at all.»

Those are not the same ethic.

The joke writes itself:

> **WWJD is a moral probe, not root access.**

4. The Greedy Integral Problem

Suppose meaningful life is represented schematically as

$$
J=\int_0^T u(t)\,dt.
$$

A tempting strategy is to maximize u(t) at every moment.

If the moments were independent, this would work.

But a life is a dynamical system.

Let

$$
x_{t+1}=F(x_t,a_t,\eta_t),
$$

where x_t includes present conditions, a_t is action, and η_t includes everything outside the agent’s control.

Now the objective is not merely

$$
\max u(x_t,a_t).
$$

It is something closer to

$$
\max_{a(\cdot)}
\int_0^T u(x_t,a_t)\,dt
$$

subject to the state dynamics.

This changes everything.

The greedy policy

$$
a_t^{G} := \arg\max_a u(x_t,a)
$$

need not maximize the integral because each apparently isolated moment changes the initial conditions of the next one.

The error can be stated compactly:

> **maximizing the integrand locally does not maximize the integral when the integrand is dynamically coupled through state.**

This is the mathematical skeleton underneath Underground Super Hans and Pavlov’s Veruca.

Hans attacks confidence in the projected trajectory.

Veruca rewards present resonance.

The resulting controller says:

«Given the world as it currently exists, choose the most livable state now.»

But the world that “currently exists” tomorrow has partly been created by today’s action.

The controller keeps inheriting its own output.

Jesus enters on a different axis: when the inherited state contains moral cost, the sacrificial sensor can volunteer the self as the corrective actuator. That can partially oppose Veruca in the short term while worsening the same coupled-state problem over longer horizons.

5. Why the future loses the argument phenomenologically

TLICA supplies a useful structural asymmetry.

The present is the active imprinting interface. Past states have actual persisted traces. Future states, by contrast, are available through Mode-B projection: simulations of possible imprinting rather than already-imprinted actuality.

Thus Future-Me possesses a peculiar phenomenological status.

She matters causally.

But presently, she is represented through a model.

This creates an asymmetry between:

> epistemic concreteness

and

> causal relevance.

Present discomfort is contact-rich.

Future constraint is simulation-rich.

If simulation is effortful, uncertain, and associated with Underground-style overthinking, the present repeatedly wins.

This is particularly important under low slack.

A sophisticated plan requires constructing counterfactual futures, transporting actions through uncertain causal models, comparing outcomes, preserving working assumptions, and then acting now in service of something not yet actual.

The demand “just plan ahead” can therefore amount to:

«Please perform expensive model-predictive control before you are allowed to live.»

The rejection of that demand is understandable.

The mistake is not rejecting exhaustive planning.

The mistake is treating model uncertainty as though it weakened the causal coupling itself.

The future is epistemically weak and causally real.

That distinction is the hinge.

There is a second hinge now visible.

The fact that suffering is weak evidence against an ontological proposition does not make it weak evidence about the controller applying that proposition.

A system can therefore be simultaneously correct that

> pain ⇏ the theorem is false

and catastrophically wrong to conclude

> pain ⇏ change the policy.

6. Why Underground Super Hans is moreish

The Hans-state is not merely intellectually self-supporting.

It is phenomenologically rewarding.

It pays immediately in several currencies.

6.1 Agency

A constraint can be reframed from:

«I am trapped.»

into:

«I refuse to pretend this deserves authority over me.»

The external problem may remain unchanged, but the experienced relation to it shifts from passive imposition to active refusal.

6.2 Epistemic superiority

The frame offers the reward:

«At least I can see that this entire arrangement is ridiculous.»

Even when no practical route is available, one can remain uncaptured at the level of interpretation.

6.3 Relief

Projected obligations lose force.

The future stops prosecuting the present.

6.4 Comedy

Perhaps most importantly, absurdity is metabolically valuable.

Once suffering becomes ridiculous, it becomes usable.

It becomes story.

It becomes bit.

It becomes material.

The world may still be bad, but now it is at least funny.

This is where the state becomes self-reinforcing:

> constraint → Hans frame → felt liberation → reduced future-oriented correction → more constraint → stronger evidence for Hans.

The frame produces conditions that make the frame increasingly appropriate.

It is not merely self-sealing.

It is self-seasoning.

The consequences improve the flavor.

This ontology is really moreish.

7. The cosmic-comedy feedback loop

The strangest part of the system is that consequences arrive temporally separated from their causes.

Present consciousness receives:

> current state,

not automatically:

> current state + complete provenance graph.

Thus the morning state may appear as:

«Why the fuck is everything like this?»

Only later does a causal chain become visible:

$$
a_{t-n}
\rightarrow
a_{t-n+1}
\rightarrow
\dots
\rightarrow
x_t.
$$

And suddenly:

«Oh.»

«Past me did some of this.»

Not all of it.

Material conditions, other people, institutions, chance, and structural constraints remain causal participants.

But enough of the chain becomes visible to trigger a peculiar recognition.

The tragedy becomes a joke with a delayed punchline.

Providential narratives and self-authorship narratives can therefore exhibit the same phenomenological form:

> opaque suffering → expanded horizon → retrospective integration → laughter.

One version says:

«God, you absolute bastard, I see what you were doing.»

The other says:

«Past me, you absolute bastard, I see what you were doing.»

The metaphysics differ.

The comedy structure does not.

8. Why ordinary “discipline” fails as a cure

The obvious intervention is:

«Plan more.»

This misses the architecture.

If exhaustive future simulation is precisely what the agent experiences as cognitively expensive, epistemically dubious, and Underground-inducing, then prescribing more planning simply demands stronger use of the mechanism that already fails under load.

TLICA’s work on steerable commitment makes an adjacent distinction: an endorsed destination is not itself an actuator. Recognizing the desired state does not yet provide a reliable intervention capable of producing it.

Likewise:

> better forecast ≠ better controller.

The relevant distinction is between an open-loop plan and a feedback policy.

An open-loop plan attempts to specify:

$$
(a_0,a_1,\dots,a_T).
$$

A feedback policy says:

$$
a_t=\pi(x_t).
$$

The latter need not correctly predict the entire future.

It only needs to respond adequately to whichever future actually arrives.

This is much closer to the original aspiration:

«construct a method of being that self-resonates and is future-transcendent.»

The phrase becomes rigorous once “future-transcendent” means:

«the policy remains useful across a broad class of futures without requiring one predicted future to occur.»

9. From future prediction to future invariants

The mature correction is therefore not to kill Super Hans, starve Veruca, or discard the sacrificial moral ideal.

All three are useful sensors.

Hans asks:

«Is this plan actually grounded, or have we started believing our own projection?»

Veruca asks:

«Is this life receiving enough actual reward to remain worth inhabiting?»

Jesus asks:

«Am I protecting myself by making somebody else bear a cost I could reasonably help carry?»

None should govern alone.

Instead, the controller should preserve a small set of invariants.

For example:

$$
\pi^*(x)=
\arg\max_a
\left[
R_{\text{present}}(x,a)
+
\lambda O(F(x,a)) - \mu I(x,a)
\right],
$$

where:

- R_{\text{present}} is current self-congruent value,
- O is future option value,
- I is irreversible downside.

This objective is schematic rather than complete: it does not yet contain an operational moral-externality term. The Jesus-sensor therefore remains a qualitative constraint here rather than being smuggled into an unearned scalar.

The crucial quantity is option value.

Conventional deferred gratification says:

«suffer now so Future You can enjoy something later.»

That framing is easy to reject.

Option preservation says:

«do not unnecessarily delete moves from Future You’s action space.»

Let

$$
\mathcal A(x_t)
$$

denote the actions available from state x_t.

Then one useful constraint is:

> **avoid gratuitous actions for which $|\mathcal A(x_{t+1})| \ll |\mathcal A(x_t)|$.**

Money matters partly because it preserves options.

Mobility preserves options.

Skills preserve options.

Sleep preserves options.

Administrative maintenance can preserve options.

Relationships can preserve options.

Avoiding unnecessary irreversible commitments preserves options.

None of these requires worshipping a specific imagined future.

They preserve freedom for an unknown one.

And preserving one's own option set is not automatically selfish. The self is one of the morally relevant systems whose future capacities enter the world.

10. Low-energy Underground

This also clarifies the role of reflection.

The goal is not zero Underground.

TLICA’s later work explicitly moves toward plural inhabited life with low-cost reflective transitions, rather than permanent self-monitoring or permanent immersion.

The useful question is not:

«What should my entire future be?»

It is:

«Is this move closing a door I may need?»

That is a much cheaper computation.

Other useful probes follow:

- If I repeat this exact policy thirty times, where does it tend?
- Is the consequence reversible?
- Am I buying relief by manufacturing obligation?
- Does this action enlarge or shrink my later action set?
- What is the smallest present action that preserves a desirable route?
- Is this forecast sufficiently reliable to justify constraining the present?
- Is the present sufficiently bad that the supposedly prudent future strategy is itself unsustainable?
- Am I treating my own distress as controller telemetry, or demanding that it prove a theorem false before I listen to it?
- Am I bearing a proportionate moral cost, or using myself as an unbounded sink for residual error?
- Did I validate a scoped observation and then silently compile it into a global rule?

These are finite heuristics.

They do not require pretending uncertainty has disappeared.

They respect the original insight.

They simply stop treating uncertainty as exemption from dynamics, sacrifice as exemption from proportionality, or phenomenological confirmation as a license to erase scope.

11. The sensors, demoted but retained

The point is not to become respectable.

Indeed, that would be an almost comically bad ending.

Underground Super Hans is too useful to remove.

He should detect fraud.

He should detect bullshit.

He should attack overconfident forecasts.

He should notice when social norms disguise somebody else’s objective function as necessity.

He should remind the system that models are models.

But:

> **Super Hans is a sensor, not a governor.**

Likewise, Pavlov’s Veruca should remain.

A life that never produces present reward is not vindicated merely because somebody has drawn an attractive arrow toward the future.

She is the system’s protest against becoming an instrument for abstractions.

But:

> **Veruca is a reward sensor, not head of capital allocation.**

And the Jesus-ideal should remain.

A morality that never asks who bears the cost of my comfort is not redeemed merely because I have learned to call self-protection “boundaries.” Sometimes the right thing really is to take the hit.

But willingness to sacrifice is not the same as assigning the self zero weight.

> **WWJD is a moral probe, not root access.**

The mature controller requires all three.

Hans keeps projection epistemically humble.

Veruca keeps lived existence phenomenologically alive.

Jesus keeps moral externalities visible.

None gets sole executive authority.

The deeper rule is now clear:

> **A sensor may reveal a truth without being entitled to become the global policy.**

---

12. Conclusion: this ontology is really moreish

The deepest error examined here is not hedonism, irresponsibility, rebellion, insufficient planning, religion, or self-sacrifice.

It is the promotion of scoped truths into global governors.

The future cannot be known completely.

It therefore cannot legitimately dominate the present merely by presenting itself as certainty.

But uncertainty about a future state does not remove the causal dependence of that state upon present action.

Present reward is real.

But present resonance does not certify that a repeated policy preserves the state from which later reward must be received.

Self-sacrifice can be morally good.

But moral seriousness does not require deleting the self from the set of beings whose suffering counts.

Phenomenological probing can uncover real structure.

But successfully noticing a pattern does not certify the broadest possible rule that can be written from it.

A finite embedded agent therefore needs neither blind deferred gratification nor total presentism, neither moral narcissism nor moral self-erasure, neither authority worship nor a phenomenological canonization machine that can only ratchet one way.

It needs an adaptive policy.

Such a policy should remain revisable under new evidence, reward actual lived existence, preserve future degrees of freedom, treat irreversible commitments with greater suspicion than reversible experiments, and allow distress to function as telemetry even when it does not amount to a logical refutation.

That is the legitimate synthesis of Underground Super Hans, Pavlov’s Veruca, and Jesus with root access revoked.

The child philosopher was not wrong to distrust prophecy.

She was not wrong to notice that attachment can produce suffering.

She was not wrong to admire sacrifice.

She was not wrong to test ideas against lived experience.

The error was in the compiler.

It erased scope.

It promoted sensors to governors.

It let phenomenological resonance install rules more easily than phenomenological suffering could uninstall them.

And once those rules started shaping the state, the resulting consequences became new input to the very controller that produced them.

The worldview seasons its own consequences.

It rewards inhabiting itself.

It is, in the strict phenomenological sense,

> **really fucking moreish.**

And that is precisely why it requires a governor.
