# Abstract

Culture-war politics is often described as a clash of values, interests, identities, or factual beliefs. Each description captures something real, but all begin too late. Before citizens can disagree about a proposition, they must construct the political object to which the proposition refers. People occupy a partly shared reality through different histories of place, class, race, religion, education, occupation, media, family, status, and lived experience. Those histories generate nonidentical representational systems. The same word, candidate, institution, or event can therefore invoke different causal structures, moral weights, identity implications, and threats in different citizens.

This paper models democratic politics as an emergent contest among such representations. A candidate's “vibe” is treated literally as a voter's compressed, affectively weighted model of the governing world that candidate appears to embody. Vibe is not irrational noise, although it is fallible: no citizen can calculate every future consequence of a candidate, so political judgment necessarily depends on cues, analogy, identity, performance, and low-dimensional summaries. The crucial boundary is that perceived coherence with a voter is not correspondence to reality. Resonance is not truth.

Political parties are then modeled as persistent labels over changing coalitions, semantic bases, and policy programs. This avoids the familiar but misleading picture of one instantaneous American “party switch.” Reconstruction, the New Deal, civil rights, Southern realignment, religious conservatism, and later polarization moved different constituencies and ideological basis vectors on different clocks. Barry Goldwater's 1981 conflict with the religious right provides a bounded case in which the public token *conservatism* remained stable while actors disputed the relational object it named.

Culture war appears when within-group semantic bandwidth rises while cross-group semantic interoperability falls, especially under strong identity sorting and moralization. Citizens use the same vocabulary but decode different worlds. That divergence has a dynamic driver: when active participation earns no legible acknowledgment, political energy migrates from slow, heterogeneous institutions into fast, homogeneous semantic communities — a civic participation–disillusionment trap in which individually rational withdrawal degrades the shared capacity for correction and intensifies the codec divergence it grew from. The proposed repair is not civility, persuasion, or midpoint bargaining. It is **constraint-closed compromise**. Each viewpoint must inherit the foreseeable downstream obligations generated by its own principles and must permanently incorporate every consequential variable emphasized by its rival that survives factual, causal, source-map, rights, and hostile-control review and clears a declared materiality threshold — at minimum the single strongest such variable. I call that set the **opposition checksum**.

The result is a bounded **cohesive total viewpoint**: not omniscience, but a representation that tracks the load-bearing verified constraints inside a declared decision boundary. Real compromise is then a search over the jointly expanded constraint surface. It may produce a midpoint, a package, a third option, one side's original policy, or no permissible compromise.

A worked abortion example illustrates the method. An abortion-rights model can acknowledge nonzero moral significance in developing human life without conceding that it always overrides bodily autonomy. An abortion-opposition model that uses public power to compel continuation of pregnancy inherits obligations concerning maternal health, childbirth, economic burden, child welfare, and enforcement. Neither side becomes coherent by erasing the variable made vivid by the other.

The paper closes by extending *This Is Water*: liberal education is not only governance of attention but potential democratic semantic infrastructure. A pluralist democracy does not require shared conclusions. It requires enough common conceptual coverage that citizens can reconstruct one another's maps, identify the true residual disagreement, and carry more of reality before deciding what should be done.

# 1. Same words, different worlds

A person says her job is **Sisyphean**. If the listener knows the myth, one adjective can transmit an entire relation:

$$
\text{labor}
+
\text{recurrence}
+
\text{temporary progress}
+
\text{reset}
+
\text{futility}.
$$

Someone else describes a workplace as *Weekend at Bernie's*. The reference can carry:

$$
\text{body still operating}
+
\text{animating purpose gone}
+
\text{participants maintaining an appearance of life}.
$$

These are not merely jokes or decorations. They are compressed pointers into shared cultural structures. The listener supplies most of the decompression from memory.

Political language works the same way, except its objects are more contested. Consider:

- freedom;
- family;
- equality;
- merit;
- safety;
- racism;
- religious liberty;
- bodily autonomy;
- law and order.

Two citizens can hear the same public token and reconstruct materially different relational objects. “Religious liberty” may mean protection of conscience from state coercion to one person and state-tolerated imposition of religious norms on others to another. “Systemic racism” may mean historically persistent institutional causation to one person and an accusation of personal guilt against people who did not individually discriminate to another. “Law and order” may mean protection from violence to one person and unequal surveillance backed by coercive institutions to another.

The political disagreement may be real. But before it can be evaluated, the parties must determine whether they are disagreeing about the same object.

This paper begins from three distinctions:

$$
\boxed{\text{shared vocabulary} \neq \text{shared representation}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{shared representation} \neq \text{agreement}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{agreement} \neq \text{truth}.}
$$

Culture war is what political disagreement increasingly looks like when a society retains a common public language after losing enough shared conceptual infrastructure to decode that language in common.

# 2. Politics as an emergent function of a population

Governing bodies are not normally designed by a small group of ideal reasoners who first derive the optimal policy and then appoint the correct administrators. They emerge through elections, parties, media, institutions, money, law, geography, turnout, and the aggregated judgments of millions of citizens.

This does not make the electorate irrational. It makes political judgment a bounded information problem.

No ordinary voter can fully calculate:

- every policy a candidate will pursue;
- all appointments and institutional effects;
- legislative bargaining;
- unknown future crises;
- second-order economic consequences;
- foreign-policy contingencies;
- personality under stress;
- corruption risk;
- the quality of thousands of subordinate decisions.

The voter therefore compresses.

A candidate becomes a low-dimensional model of a possible governing world:

$$
c
\mapsto
\{
\text{one of us},
\text{strong},
\text{fair},
\text{fake},
\text{competent},
\text{elite},
\text{dangerous},
\text{corrupt},
\text{caring},
\text{able to restore order}
\}.
$$

The resulting integrated impression is what ordinary language calls a **vibe**.

Political science already contains important neighboring accounts. *The American Voter* analyzes party identification, candidate perception, issue concepts, social groups, and the limited ideological organization of many citizens. Samuel Popkin's *The Reasoning Voter* develops information shortcuts and political symbols. Gabriel Lenz questions the simple assumption that citizens first hold stable policy positions and then select aligned leaders. Kinder and Kalmoe argue that mass partisanship should not be mistaken for a uniformly ideological public.

The present claim is narrower than a new theory of voting:

> **Vibe is a real representational compression layer: the low-dimensional, affectively weighted model of a candidate's governing world that a voter actually decodes. Whether that model *resonates* — coheres with the voter's own world — is a distinct quantity, and neither is the same as whether it is true.**

The candidate can appear to voice a perceived truth that resonates with a voting body even when the message is false proper. This gives politics two quantities that must remain separate:

$$
\boxed{
\text{representational resonance}
\neq
\text{epistemic correspondence}.
}
$$

A democracy contains machinery for measuring the first: elections. It requires journalism, science, historical memory, education, adversarial institutions, and source discipline to couple the first reliably to the second.

## 2.1 A schematic model

Let $\mathcal W_t$ be the politically relevant world-state at time $t$. Person $i$ has a lived history $h_i$, environment $e_i$, social position $s_i$, and conceptual toolkit $B_i$. These contribute to a learned map:

$$
L_i:(\mathcal W_t,h_i,e_i,s_i,B_i)\rightarrow r_i(t),
$$

where $r_i(t)$ is the person's current representation of the political world.

Candidate $c$ emits a compound signal $m_c$: speech, policies, accent, style, symbols, biography, affiliations, enemies, status cues, and behavior. Person $i$ decodes it through a private basis:

$$
\widehat M_{ic}=D_i(m_c;B_i,h_i,s_i).
$$

Define a schematic resonance functional:

$$
R_i(c)
=
\mathrm{Coh}
\bigl(
\widehat M_{ic},
 r_i(t),
 v_i,
 \rho_i,
 a_i
\bigr),
$$

where $v_i$ represents values, $\rho_i$ identity or commitment coupling, and $a_i$ the current affective and material state. $\mathrm{Coh}$ is experienced fit, not truth.

In these terms, person $i$'s **vibe** for candidate $c$ is $\widehat M_{ic}$ — the decoded, affectively weighted model — and its **resonance** is $R_i(c)$, the experienced coherence of that model with the voter. The vibe is the representation; the resonance is the scalar fit computed over it. A single vibe can resonate strongly with one voter and weakly with another; that is why the two must not be collapsed into one quantity.

Support then depends on resonance together with policy, performance, institutions, social pressure, and many other variables:

$$
\Pr_i(c)=f(R_i(c),p_i,q_i,n_i,\ldots).
$$

Nothing here says voters are passive. Citizens can investigate, compare, revise, and reject cues. The model says only that political information becomes actionable through a representation rather than arriving as raw world-state.

## 2.2 The candidate as semantic multiplexing device

An office-holder must represent millions of nonidentical minds. Successful political language therefore becomes multiplexed.

The word “freedom” may be decoded as:

- freedom from taxation;
- freedom from discrimination;
- freedom to worship;
- freedom from religious imposition;
- freedom to own weapons;
- freedom from violence;
- freedom to unionize;
- freedom to refuse collective obligations.

A candidate can bind several constituencies by emitting a token underdetermined enough to support several favorable decodings.

This ambiguity is not always cynical. Coalitions require common symbols. But it creates a structural opportunity for divergent groups to believe they have authorized the same governing project when they have authorized different internal projections onto one public carrier.

## 2.3 Relation to the TLICA coordinates

This paper uses the TLICA foundation as a lens, not as a derivation. Its three coordinates map onto the machinery above:

- **φ (truth-status).** φ tracks whether a representation corresponds to what is the case. The paper's central boundary — *resonance is not truth* — is exactly the gap between experienced fit and φ. Resonance $R_i(c)$ is not φ: it is coherence with the voter, and it can run high while φ runs low.
- **κ (contact).** κ tracks whether a representation actually makes contact with the world-state it purports to model. A vibe with high resonance and low κ — a compelling model that touches nothing real — is the characteristic democratic failure this paper studies.
- **ρ (identity-correlation).** ρ already appears above as $\rho_i$ in the resonance functional: how far a candidate's model is coupled to the voter's identity and commitments.

The honest status is **TLICA-adjacent, not TLICA-derived.** Unlike a strict application paper, this one introduces apparatus the foundation does not contain — vibe, the opposition checksum, constraint closure, semantic interoperability — so it does not claim the phenomenon *falls out* of the coordinates with no new commitments. It borrows the coordinates to locate its objects and builds representational-political machinery on top. The formal predictions remain **UNVERIFIED**.

**On "source-map."** The *source-map* gate invoked throughout (§5.2, §8.2, §12.1) is not a foundation coordinate. It is this paper's name for a source-reliability concern the archive already carries at the application level — the source-attribution guardrails of *This Is Water* (its §7) and *Out of the Cave*'s re-diagnosis of the prisoners' condition as systematic source-error rather than mere false belief. Source-map review is that same discipline applied to contested political objects: a check on whether the authorities, histories, and causal maps a specific variable rests on withstand scrutiny.

# 3. Elite-coded and folk-coded political language

The motivating conversation initially used “high-class vibes” and “low-class vibes.” Taken literally, that language obscures more than it reveals. Southern politics after the Civil War, for example, included a powerful planter elite; popular and elite interests were not identical.

The useful residual is a contrast among political **representational styles**.

An elite-coded style often privileges:

- abstractions and formal categories;
- institutions and procedural legitimacy;
- statistics, expertise, and long causal chains;
- credentialed authority;
- cosmopolitan or nationally standardized language.

A folk- or populist-coded style often privileges:

- concrete agents and visible harms;
- testimony and local knowledge;
- loyalty, betrayal, humiliation, heroes, and villains;
- culturally familiar stories and symbols;
- authenticity and suspicion of distant abstraction.

Neither style is epistemically sufficient.

Elite models can discard:

- embodiment;
- local causal knowledge;
- unequal burden;
- humiliation;
- lived feasibility;
- information that does not fit institutional categories.

Folk models can discard:

- base rates;
- structural causation;
- long time horizons;
- institutional constraints;
- diffuse externalities;
- counterfactual comparison.

The democratic question should not be “Which style is the intelligent one?” It should be:

> **What consequential information does each representation preserve, and what does it throw away?**

That question is continuous with the author's wider artistic concern: recovering load-bearing structure lost through ordinary compression.

# 4. Parties as carriers, not timeless persons

Political parties have names, but a name does not fix the coalition, ideology, moral grammar, or policy program that occupies it.

Represent party $j$ at time $t$ as:

$$
P_j(t)
=
\bigl(
\ell_j,
K_j(t),
B_j(t),
\Pi_j(t)
\bigr),
$$

where:

- $\ell_j$ is the persistent label;
- $K_j(t)$ is the coalition;
- $B_j(t)$ is the operative semantic and ideological basis;
- $\Pi_j(t)$ is the policy and institutional program.

Then the label can remain constant while the object changes:

$$
\ell_j(t_1)=\ell_j(t_2)
$$

but:

$$
K_j(t_1)\neq K_j(t_2),
\qquad
B_j(t_1)\neq B_j(t_2),
\qquad
\Pi_j(t_1)\neq \Pi_j(t_2).
$$

This is a better model of American realignment than a single moment in which Democrats and Republicans exchanged souls.

## 4.1 A bounded historical sketch

During Reconstruction, the national Republican coalition carried Union victory, emancipation's political consequences, federal enforcement, and Black political participation. White Southern Democrats became the primary vehicle for restoring white local rule and the post-Confederate racial order.

During the New Deal, many Black voters moved toward the Democratic Party in response to material relief and political opportunity in northern cities, while powerful Southern segregationist Democrats remained inside the same national coalition. The U.S. House historical archive documents both the gradual Black partisan movement and the power of Southern Democrats to narrow New Deal benefits and obstruct civil-rights measures.

During the civil-rights era, the national Democratic Party increasingly supported federal enforcement while Southern Democrats led much of the opposition. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 passed through a bipartisan coalition. The National Archives documents the Southern filibuster and the indispensable Republican support organized by Senate Minority Leader Everett Dirksen. Goldwater opposed the Act and carried five Deep South states, but Southern white realignment continued over decades rather than completing in one election.

Different vectors moved on different clocks:

- race and federal enforcement;
- region;
- labor and capital;
- religion;
- urbanization;
- class and education;
- foreign policy;
- social morality;
- trust in institutions.

The useful historical question is not:

> Were the parties then secretly the parties now?

It is:

> **Which basis vectors occupied each party, how did the vectors themselves change, and where did their constituencies migrate?**

## 4.2 Goldwater and semantic drift under “conservative”

Barry Goldwater is a particularly useful internal calibration. He helped make conservative Republican politics electorally powerful, opposed the Civil Rights Act of 1964, and appealed to Southern white backlash. That history should not be softened.

Yet in a September 15, 1981 Senate speech, he attacked religious factions that pressured officials to follow their positions completely and tried to dictate moral convictions “in the name of conservatism.” The [official Congressional Record](https://www.congress.gov/bound-congressional-record/1981/09/15/127/senate-section/article/20533-20596) preserves that day's Senate proceedings; contemporary [Washington Post reporting](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1981/09/16/goldwater-lashes-religious-pressure/b1caa379-49fa-4e04-82de-dccda6f5e7f9/) records his claim that the “New Right” was not his conservatism and his fear that religious factionalism could damage representative government.

This does not prove that Goldwater predicted every later Republican development. It proves something narrower and more useful:

$$
\boxed{
\text{the lexical token “conservative” remained stable while actors contested its internal relational structure.}
}
$$

Goldwater's older grammar emphasized limited government and private liberty. The religious-right grammar could treat some moral truths as requiring public enforcement and political compromise as moral surrender. Both inhabited the same party and used the same label.

That is semantic-basis drift in historical view.

# 5. Culture war as codec divergence

Every person constructs a political object through a basis:

$$
x
\xrightarrow{B_i}
\bigl(
\text{meaning},
\text{cause},
\text{moral weight},
\text{identity relevance},
\text{threat}
\bigr)_i.
$$

Culture war intensifies when:

1. groups inhabit divergent media, histories, authorities, and examples;
2. party identity aligns with race, religion, region, education, and culture;
3. political objects become morally and personally identity-coupled;
4. in-group compression becomes efficient while out-group reconstruction becomes poor;
5. politicians and media profit from loading threat and tribe through compact symbols.

Research on affective polarization supports nearby claims. Iyengar and colleagues describe increasing dislike and distrust of the opposing party. Mason and Kalmoe emphasize the stacking of partisan identity with other social identities. Garrett and Bankert find that moral conviction can heighten partisan hostility.

The semantic contribution is this:

$$
\boxed{
\text{culture war}
\approx
\text{identity-coupled conflict among increasingly noninteroperable representations}.
}
$$

This is not a complete explanation. Sometimes two groups understand one another perfectly and still want incompatible outcomes.

## 5.1 Within-group bandwidth and cross-group failure

A meme, slogan, or political label can be a high-bandwidth callable object inside one community. The same token may carry little or distorted information across communities.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{within-group bandwidth}\uparrow
\quad\text{can coexist with}\quad
\text{cross-group interoperability}\downarrow.
}
$$

One side says “woke” and loads a model of coercive elite moral fashion, institutional capture, and contempt for ordinary people. Another hears the same word as a dismissive attack on awareness of injustice. One side says “fascist” and loads authoritarian hierarchy, dehumanization, and political violence. Another hears a generic insult for anyone to the right of the speaker.

The public debate then becomes recursive:

$$
A
\rightarrow
D_B(A)
\rightarrow
D_A(D_B(A))
\rightarrow
\cdots
$$

Each coalition reacts to its model of the other coalition's model of itself.

## 5.2 Five obstructions hidden inside one argument

Culture-war conflict should be decomposed before anyone proposes a solution.

| Obstruction | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|
| **Codec gap** | Are the parties reconstructing one another's intended claim? |
| **Source-map gap** | Do they rely on different authorities, histories, or causal maps? |
| **Empirical gap** | Which propositions survive evidence and measurement? |
| **Value gap** | Do they understand the facts but rank rights or outcomes differently? |
| **Power/strategy gap** | Is one actor protecting advantage, bargaining in bad faith, or using dialogue instrumentally? |

Translation repairs only the first. It may expose the others more clearly.

# 6. The civic participation–disillusionment trap

The codec divergence of §5 has a dynamic engine. Culture-war migration is not only a matter of divergent semantic bases held statically; it is also driven by *failed civic reinforcement* — participating in heterogeneous institutions and receiving no legible acknowledgment that the effort changed anything. This section models that engine and its repair. The dynamics and behavioral predictions below are **UNVERIFIED**; they are stated to be tested (§13), not asserted.

Democratic participation is costly, its effects are diffuse, and its causal feedback is delayed and hard to attribute. A citizen who votes, volunteers, organizes, testifies, or repeatedly defends a coalition may receive no receipt reading *this action of yours caused this improvement*. Active participation also raises exposure to incompetence, betrayal, defeat, and the humiliation of having believed a party or institution would act differently. Political efficacy can therefore decline through experience rather than ignorance. Withdrawal — into abstention, anti-system rejection, or a culturally sealed community — becomes individually intelligible.

But withdrawal changes the environment. Broad exit weakens monitoring, competition, institutional memory, and corrective pressure; concentrated and organized actors gain relative power; parties face lower costs for serving insiders or invoking the opposing coalition as a permanent emergency. Institutional quality declines, producing fresh evidence that participation was futile. The loop can close on itself:

$$
\boxed{
\text{participation}
\rightarrow
\text{exposure to failure}
\rightarrow
\text{efficacy loss}
\rightarrow
\text{exit or tribal retreat}
\rightarrow
\text{weaker correction}
\rightarrow
\text{worse institutions}
\rightarrow
\text{confirmed cynicism}
}
$$

The trap is not evidence that citizens are irrational. It is a case in which individually intelligible adaptations aggregate into a collectively destructive equilibrium. The author informally calls the private version the *New York Jets effect*: sustained attachment repeatedly exposes a person to disappointment until the self-protective move is to stop investing.

Withdrawal, though, is not uniform, and the asymmetry is load-bearing. The effect empties the *casual* tier first — the bandwagon supporter stops watching and is simply gone. The committed core behaves differently: a true fan pulls back from costly active investment yet keeps a pilot light burning — still rooting, still hoping, still acting when the moment is cheap and legible enough to act on. In the exit–voice–loyalty terms of §6.1 that is loyalty holding the line: it does not stop voice from thinning, but it forestalls full exit and keeps a channel that can reopen. That residual — reduced voice, retained loyalty, episodic action when it can matter — is the naturally occurring form of the *conditional civic loyalty* the repair below targets (§6.4), and evidence that "disillusioned but not gone" is a real, occupied position rather than a normative ideal. A polity sliding toward the cynical equilibrium (§6.2) is therefore rarely empty of corrective capacity; it runs on its true fans while the general public exits — which makes the repair less a matter of manufacturing loyalty from nothing than of widening that committed core and giving its episodic action legible traction.

The analogy has a limit that carries the whole argument — a fan's withdrawal does not degrade the team, but a citizen's withdrawal can reduce the monitoring, competition, and corrective pressure available to democratic government, and so help produce the very condition that made exit seem necessary.

## 6.1 A coupled system, not one game

The structure resembles a Prisoner's Dilemma but does not reduce to one two-player game. The faithful object is a coupled system of at least four mechanisms, each supplying a distinct failure mode:

- an $N$-person **public-goods** problem — broad democratic correction is non-excludable, so each citizen is tempted to let others pay the participation cost;
- an **exit–voice–loyalty** problem — too little loyalty produces premature exit before voice can operate; too much protects leadership from consequences and turns decline into impunity;
- a **principal–agent** problem — unconditional support lowers the cost of organizational defection, while total exit cedes the institution to actors with more concentrated incentives;
- a **delayed-reinforcement** problem — high immediate cost, weak visible reward, and uncertain attribution teach an ordinary learner to *stop investing*, even where useful effects appear only after many weak contributions accumulate.

No actor need have designed the system for it to settle into a low-trust equilibrium.

## 6.2 Two equilibria

The sign structure admits two self-reinforcing regimes — causal loops, not a formal stability analysis. A **corrective** regime,

$$
P_{\text{broad}}\uparrow
\rightarrow
\text{monitoring, competition, replacement}\uparrow
\rightarrow
Q\uparrow
\rightarrow
A\uparrow
\rightarrow
E\uparrow
\rightarrow
P_{\text{broad}}\uparrow,
$$

where $P_{\text{broad}}$ is broad participation, $Q$ institutional quality, $A$ legible acknowledgment, and $E$ perceived efficacy. It does not require universal trust — only enough citizens remaining in correction channels long enough for failure to produce replacement or adaptation. And a **cynical** equilibrium,

$$
P_{\text{broad}}\downarrow
\rightarrow
C\uparrow
\rightarrow
Q\downarrow
\rightarrow
F\uparrow
\rightarrow
E\downarrow
\rightarrow
P_{\text{broad}}\downarrow,
$$

where $C$ is capture by narrow interests and $F$ observed failure. It is stable because every new failure supplies evidence for disengagement while disengagement increases the power of actors who benefit from weakened correction. The second equilibrium can be locally rational for many citizens and globally damaging.

## 6.3 Failed acknowledgment and culture-war migration

The connection to §5 is not incidental. When institutional voice returns no legible acknowledgment, an aligned cultural community can offer a far faster response — immediate recognition, shared language, clear villains, identity reinforcement:

$$
\text{voice}\rightarrow\text{no legible ACK}
\qquad\text{versus}\qquad
\text{voice}\rightarrow\text{immediate cultural ACK}.
$$

Participation therefore migrates from slow, procedurally constrained, heterogeneous institutions toward fast, homogeneous semantic communities. Those communities supply real goods: recognition, intelligibility, solidarity, reusable narratives, and an explanation for institutional failure. But the migration is precisely the mechanism that drives the bandwidth asymmetry of §5.1:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{institutional nonresponse}
\rightarrow \text{retreat into aligned culture}\\
&\rightarrow \text{within-group semantic bandwidth}\uparrow
\rightarrow \text{cross-group interoperability}\downarrow\\
&\rightarrow \text{coalition governance harder}
\rightarrow \text{institutional failure}\uparrow
\rightarrow \text{nonresponse confirmed}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Culture war can thus be partly downstream of failed civic reinforcement: a substitute participation system, highly active as identity and communication, weakly coupled to corrigible common institutions. The load-bearing distinction is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{political intensity}
\neq
\text{civic capacity}.
}
$$

A population can become more politically expressive while becoming less able to maintain mutually interpretable, correctable institutions.

## 6.4 Conditional civic loyalty as a constraint-closed repair

The repair is neither blind faith nor total exit, and it is an instance of the method this paper formalizes in §8. First, four commitments that ordinary politics fuses must be separated:

$$
\boxed{
\text{democratic participation}
\neq
\text{party loyalty}
\neq
\text{emotional identification}
\neq
\text{confidence in near-term victory}.
}
$$

A citizen can participate while judging the available party deeply defective, remain committed to correction without making an organization part of the self, continue after losses without pretending victory is imminent, and withdraw support from one agent without abandoning the civic system. Fusing these produces two symmetrical collapses: unconditional loyalty (participation $+$ identity fusion $\rightarrow$ party impunity), and total exit (one betrayal $\rightarrow$ rejection of participation as such).

The target is **conditional civic loyalty**: keep some corrective voice active across delayed feedback, while holding every party, leader, tactic, and institution answerable to evidence and results. Under constraint closure (§8), each pole must absorb the variable it is tempted to discard. The civic-faith position's opposition checksum is:

> **Participation that receives no credible causal acknowledgment and imposes repeated asymmetric costs can rationally decay; institutions inherit an obligation to make voice more legible, consequential, and corrigible.**

The anti-system position's opposition checksum is:

> **Exit can help produce the unresponsive institution it correctly diagnoses; justified rejection of one agent must not automatically terminate every corrective channel.**

The jointly closed position is therefore neither optimism nor cynicism:

$$
\boxed{
\text{warranted distrust}
+
\text{continued conditional voice}
+
\text{agent accountability}
+
\text{institutional receipts}
}
$$

Institutions inherit the reciprocal duty the first checksum names — publish what public comment changed, expose decision pathways, and build channels where participation alters outcomes rather than decorating them — because a fast cultural acknowledgment that validates identity while reinforcing a false causal model is not a substitute for a real receipt. A democracy may need enough faith to bridge delayed collective feedback, but that faith belongs to the *possibility of correction*, not to any organization claiming to embody it:

$$
\boxed{
\text{faith in democratic correction}
\neq
\text{faithfulness to a party}.
}
$$

## 6.5 Robustness caveats

The model must not moralize citizens or fetishize participation. Withdrawal can be fully rational under high cost, low access, trauma, disability, or retaliation risk, and more participation is not automatically better — participation can serve authoritarian, exclusionary, or corrupt projects, so the target is *truth-disciplined, rights-bounded, corrigible civic capacity*, not maximal mobilization. High trust in an untrustworthy institution is a defect, not a goal. Conditional loyalty can itself decay into permanent lesser-evil capture when a party converts every election into an emergency that blocks internal accountability; it therefore requires real thresholds and replacement routes, not private dissatisfaction followed by automatic support. Voice can be counterfeit: an institution can solicit input it never lets affect a decision, and visibly noncausal consultation may reduce efficacy more than no invitation at all. Finally, semantic and reinforcement analysis cannot replace material analysis — a citizen's failure to move government may reflect domination, legal exclusion, or economic asymmetry rather than a missing receipt.

# 7. Why ordinary compromise feels fake

Political compromise is often imagined as splitting the distance:

$$
\pi_{\mathrm{mid}}
=
\lambda\pi_A+(1-\lambda)\pi_B.
$$

This can be useful when the parties agree on the object and differ only over a scalar quantity. It becomes incoherent when the starting positions are incomplete representations.

Suppose side A tracks $X$ and side B tracks $Y$. Each treats the other's omission as proof of moral corruption:

$$
A:\quad \text{How can you ignore }X?
$$

$$
B:\quad \text{How can you ignore }Y?
$$

A midpoint between the slogans may continue to ignore both causal structures.

Real compromise requires a prior representational event:

$$
X\cup Y
\rightarrow
\text{shared expanded model}.
$$

Only then can the parties locate whether the residual disagreement concerns evidence, weights, rights, feasibility, interests, or implementation.

The key move is not:

> I will surrender twenty percent if you surrender twenty percent.

It is:

> **Your perspective revealed a variable my model was undertracking. I will keep that variable even when it becomes inconvenient to my preferred conclusion.**

That is actual model revision.

# 8. Cohesive total viewpoints

The intuitive target is a **cohesive total viewpoint**: a political representation that does not maintain coherence by throwing away the people, consequences, and obligations generated by its own principles.

Literal totality is impossible. Every model compresses. The operational concept must therefore be bounded.

## 8.1 Bounded constraint closure

Let a viewpoint be:

$$
W=(P,F,V,\Pi),
$$

where:

- $P$ = declared principles;
- $F$ = factual and causal map;
- $V$ = value ranking;
- $\Pi$ = recommended policy.

For a declared boundary $\Omega$, define:

$$
\mathrm{Cl}_{\Omega}(W)
=
\text{the foreseeable commitments and obligations generated by applying }P
\text{ consistently to }F\text{ within }\Omega.
$$

A viewpoint is **constraint-closed relative to $\Omega$** when it does not stop causal or moral reasoning at the politically convenient edge of its preferred policy.

The boundary must state:

- affected populations;
- time horizon;
- materiality threshold;
- uncertainty;
- institutional assumptions;
- excluded effects and why they are excluded.

Constraint closure does not mean every consequence has equal moral weight. It means every load-bearing consequence must appear in the model before weighting occurs.

## 8.2 The opposition checksum

Each side must identify the strongest valid variables its opponent tracks that it is tempted to discard.

A candidate variable is **valid** when it survives five gates:

- **factual** — it names a real state of affairs, not a fabrication;
- **causal** — it is actually connected to the outcomes at issue, not merely associated with them;
- **source-map** — the authorities, histories, and causal maps it rests on withstand scrutiny (§5.2);
- **rights** — it does not require erasing another party's basic standing (§12.2);
- **hostile-control** — its operative content is a consequence to be weighed, not a demand for one party to coerce, dominate, or decide *for* another. A variable fails this gate when, decoded honestly, it reduces to "our group should control yours" rather than "this real effect should count." A demand for domination does not become a legitimate constraint by being sincerely held.

Write $\mathrm{Valid}(x)$ for passing all five gates. For rival viewpoints $W_A$ and $W_B$, and the materiality threshold $\tau_\Omega$ declared in the boundary $\Omega$ (§8.1), the opposition checksum is the **set** of the rival's discarded valid variables that clear that threshold:

$$
\mathrm{OC}_{B\rightarrow A}
=
\bigl\{\,
x\in X_B\setminus X_A
\;:\;
\mathrm{Valid}(x)\ \land\ \mathrm{Impact}(x)\ge \tau_\Omega
\,\bigr\}.
$$

The single strongest member,

$$
\arg\max_{x\in \mathrm{OC}_{B\rightarrow A}}\mathrm{Impact}(x),
$$

is the **floor**: the one variable a side is least entitled to omit. Where the abstract and the worked examples speak of "the opposition checksum" in the singular, they name that load-bearing floor; the full set is every discarded rival variable above threshold.

The opposition checksum is not whatever the opponent sincerely believes. A fabrication, a dehumanizing assumption, or a demand for domination fails the gates above and does not earn inclusion through repetition.

The checksum asks:

> **What is the strongest real variable the rival model notices that mine would become less truthful or less coherent by continuing to omit?**

## 8.3 Constraint-closed compromise

Construct the validated joint constraint set:

$$
\mathcal C^\ast
=
\mathrm{Valid}
\left(
\mathrm{Cl}_{\Omega}(W_A)
\cup
\mathrm{Cl}_{\Omega}(W_B)
\cup
\mathrm{OC}_{B\rightarrow A}
\cup
\mathrm{OC}_{A\rightarrow B}
\right).
$$

A constraint-closed compromise is a feasible policy selected inside the region induced by $\mathcal C^\ast$, with unresolved value conflicts stated rather than concealed.

The method can produce:

- overlap;
- a package of reciprocal obligations;
- a third solution;
- one side's original policy after the rival variable is refuted;
- no permissible compromise.

The governing principle is:

> **The task is not to make every side think the same thing. It is to make every side carry more of reality.**

## 8.4 A practical protocol

Before bargaining, each side should answer:

1. What is my principle?
2. What facts and causal relations does it depend on?
3. Who bears its direct and downstream burdens?
4. What foreseeable obligation does the principle generate after my preferred policy is enacted?
5. What variable does the opposing side track that my current model omits?
6. Does that variable survive factual and rights review?
7. What evidence would narrow or falsify my policy?
8. What constraints remain genuinely incompatible after the model is expanded?

This is not a civility ritual. It is an epistemic and moral accounting procedure.

# 9. Worked example: abortion

Abortion is a useful test because ordinary discourse often maintains each position by maximizing one vivid variable and minimizing another.

Use the labels **abortion-rights viewpoint** and **abortion-opposition viewpoint**. “Pro-abortion” often misstates a position that defends legal access without treating abortion as intrinsically desirable.

This section does not derive one legal answer.

## 9.1 The abortion-rights basis

Abortion-rights arguments commonly track:

- bodily integrity and autonomy;
- health and mortality risk;
- privacy and medical decision-making;
- rape, abuse, and coercion;
- unequal economic and social burdens;
- gestational stage;
- miscarriage and emergency-care spillovers;
- surveillance and unequal enforcement;
- the difference between moral concern and compulsory bodily use.

These variables are real. A political model that treats pregnancy as a disembodied moral relation throws away the person whose body is carrying it.

## 9.2 The abortion-opposition basis

Abortion-opposition arguments commonly track:

- the moral significance of developing human life;
- continuity of development;
- duties toward radically dependent life;
- parental and social responsibility;
- gestational development, viability, and sentience;
- moral injury and regret;
- the claim that autonomy is limited when another morally relevant being is involved.

These variables cannot be dismissed merely by changing vocabulary. A political model that treats developing life as morally zero owes a serious argument for that value assignment.

## 9.3 Closure for an abortion-rights viewpoint

An abortion-rights model can coherently state:

$$
\text{developing-life moral significance}>0
$$

while still holding:

$$
\text{that significance does not always override bodily autonomy}.
$$

Constraint closure requires the model to explain how gestational development, viability, sentience, dependence, and prospective personhood affect moral weight, if at all. It also encourages policies that reduce unwanted pregnancy and make wanted pregnancy safer without assuming that legal prohibition is the only way to value life.

The concession is not:

> The opposition is right about the legal conclusion.

It is:

> **The opposition is tracking a morally serious variable that my model should not erase.**

## 9.4 Closure for an abortion-opposition viewpoint

An abortion-opposition model does not become coherent when a prohibition is signed.

If public power compels continuation of pregnancy, it inherits downstream obligations concerning:

- prenatal and childbirth care;
- maternal health and disability;
- income loss and employment protection;
- parental leave;
- childcare;
- infant and child healthcare;
- food and housing security;
- disability support;
- adoption and foster-system capacity;
- the burdens of investigation and enforcement;
- medical exceptions and emergency uncertainty.

The exact allocation of those obligations—state, family, community, insurance, charity, or a mixture—remains a political question. But a viewpoint cannot treat developing life as priceless until delivery and then privatize every consequence created by compelled birth without owing an additional argument.

The principle generalizes. If vulnerable human life is morally important, post-birth welfare is not an unrelated issue. A coherent position must track children and families after birth generally, not only pregnancies subjected to legal compulsion.

## 9.5 The expanded shared object

A more cohesive model can contain:

$$
\boxed{
\text{developing-life moral weight}>0
\quad\land\quad
\text{bodily autonomy}>0
\quad\land\quad
\text{maternal and child welfare obligations}>0.
}
$$

The remaining disagreement becomes more honest:

- What are the empirical facts?
- How should moral weight change through gestation?
- What rights may the state enforce?
- Which exceptions are required?
- What enforcement mechanisms create intolerable harms?
- Who pays the material costs?
- Which institutions are competent?
- What uncertainty should block coercive intervention?

Real compromise may then include policy packages rather than one scalar line: pregnancy prevention, healthcare, material support, gestational distinctions, emergency protections, enforcement limits, and child welfare. The framework does not specify the package. It specifies what may no longer be omitted while constructing one.

## 9.6 What the example demonstrates

The example's result is methodological:

> **No side earns coherence by tracking one morally vivid variable while discarding the foreseeable persons, burdens, and obligations generated by its own principle.**

This is stronger than “both sides have a point” and weaker than a final moral theory.

# 10. Liberal education as democratic semantic infrastructure

David Foster Wallace's *This Is Water* is often reduced to compassion or mindfulness. Its deeper practical structure concerns the ability to notice a default frame and make attention and interpretation contestable before an ordinary episode closes.

The existing TLICA application paper reconstructs liberal education into three functions:

1. exposing the partiality of an apparently self-evident frame;
2. making attention and interpretation available as objects of choice;
3. supporting the repeated practice needed for that capacity to survive ordinary pressure.

An author-level extension adds a fourth:

4. **building shared conceptual infrastructure that increases the expected fidelity of perspective transfer among differently situated minds.**

*Transfer* here is inter-mind — the fidelity with which one person's perspective reconstructs in another. It is distinct from the intra-mind epistemic-to-agential *transport* of *This Is Water*, which moves a consideration from availability to timely exercise inside a single mind.

Literature, history, philosophy, mathematics, science, political thought, religion, rhetoric, and art do not merely make one person “well rounded.” They install reusable objects, distinctions, counterexamples, and analogies.

Two people who know Sisyphus can communicate recurring futile labor in one word. Two people who know Plato's cave can communicate a family of closure and source-map problems. Statistical concepts can make base rates, regression, selection effects, and uncertainty discussable. Historical knowledge can stop every conflict from appearing unprecedented.

A liberal education is not literally the sum of human knowledge. The stronger model is:

> **Transmit a compact, generative subset of accumulated inquiry that provides high coverage over recurring structures and efficient pointers into the larger archive.**

This creates semantic interoperability:

$$
\text{shared conceptual coverage}
\rightarrow
\text{lower translation cost}
\rightarrow
\text{higher-probability faithful reconstruction}.
$$

The result is not agreement and not truth by consensus. It is the possibility of locating the real residual disagreement.

## 10.1 Why degrees are not the construct

A college degree is at most a noisy proxy. A person can graduate without retaining a usable shared library. Another can build one independently.

The target variable is actual conceptual coverage and transfer capacity, not credential status.

## 10.2 Why a common canon can still fail

A standardized education can create efficient communication inside a shared false map. It can omit women, working-class experience, colonized peoples, disabled persons, non-Western traditions, or any perspective excluded by the institution constructing the canon.

Shared infrastructure can produce both:

$$
\text{semantic interoperability}
$$

and:

$$
\text{correlated compression loss}.
$$

The canon must therefore be revisable and plural. Its purpose is not to close the conceptual world but to give citizens enough common coordinates to inspect, criticize, and expand it together.

# 11. Prior art and the proposed residual

The paper sits near several mature literatures. Academic novelty is **UNVERIFIED**.

## 11.1 Voting heuristics

Political behavior research already shows that voters rely on party identity, social groups, candidate traits, symbols, and information shortcuts. The present paper's “vibe” language is a representational synthesis of those phenomena, not a claim to have discovered heuristic voting.

## 11.2 Moral reframing

Matthew Feinberg and Robb Willer show that political arguments are often more persuasive when framed in the moral language of the intended audience.

Moral reframing asks:

> How can I express my conclusion inside your moral basis?

Constraint closure asks:

> **What real variable in your basis reveals an omission in mine, and what happens if I permanently incorporate it before deciding whether my conclusion survives?**

Reframing can improve transfer while leaving the sender's model unchanged. Constraint closure makes model revision part of the success condition.

## 11.3 Deliberation and perspective-taking

Research on deliberation, perspective-taking, cross-partisan empathy, and cross-partisan conversation suggests that structured encounters can improve reflective judgment or reduce animosity under some conditions.

The proposed residual is an explicit object to transfer:

1. reconstruct the opponent's model;
2. identify the load-bearing omitted variable;
3. validate the variable;
4. close one's own model under the resulting commitment;
5. search the expanded solution space.

## 11.4 Integrative complexity

Research on integrative complexity distinguishes simple, rigid processing from differentiated and integrated treatment of competing dimensions.

Constraint closure is adjacent but not identical. A speaker can sound highly complex while omitting one decisive burden. A constraint-closed position may become simple after the correct variables are included.

## 11.5 Overlapping consensus

Rawlsian overlapping consensus and Habermasian deliberation address how citizens with different doctrines can justify shared political arrangements. This paper works one layer earlier: whether citizens possess enough semantic interoperability to reconstruct the object and enough constraint closure to state their actual commitments before public justification begins.

## 11.6 Candidate contribution

The repository-level contribution is the synthesis:

1. private learned political bases;
2. candidate vibe as a compressed, affectively weighted world-model, and its resonance with a voter;
3. parties as persistent labels over changing coalition and semantic content;
4. culture war as identity-coupled codec divergence;
5. bounded closure under each side's downstream obligations;
6. an opposition checksum that must survive validation and then remain in the model;
7. compromise as search over the jointly expanded constraint surface.

The strongest potentially distinctive seam is items 5–7. Items 4–7 are exercised on two worked cases: abortion (§9), a static moral conflict, and the civic participation–disillusionment trap (§6), a dynamic one in which failed acknowledgment feeds the codec divergence of item 4.

# 12. Boundaries: where compromise should stop

A framework for compromise becomes dangerous if it treats every conflict as symmetrical and every demand as negotiable.

## 12.1 False balance

Two public positions do not automatically possess equal evidence. The opposition checksum must survive the validation gates of §8.2 — factual, causal, source-map, rights, and hostile-control review.

## 12.2 Standing and rights

A person's basic standing cannot be treated as one negotiable preference among others. Some proposals fail because they erase persons, demand domination, or remove equal protection.

This paper does not provide a complete rights theory. It states that semantic sincerity cannot excuse dehumanization.

## 12.3 Power asymmetry

A dominant group can demand “dialogue” while retaining control of law, property, employment, force, or agenda-setting. A complete model must track:

- who can exit;
- who bears delay;
- who pays the costs;
- who controls enforcement;
- who defines the options;
- who is safe while deliberation continues.

## 12.4 Bad faith and propaganda

A political entrepreneur can learn another group's semantic basis in order to manipulate it. High interoperability can make propaganda more efficient.

Propaganda need not be authored, however. The procedural-justice *voice effect* — being invited to speak raises a participant's sense that a process was fair even when the input changed nothing — shows that a legitimacy signal can decouple from real causal influence as an **emergent** property of participation itself, with no designer. An institution can thereby run counterfeit consultation (§6.5) that *feels* responsive in the moment, manufacturing perceived legitimacy that tracks no actual influence: emergent, accidental propaganda, reproducing the characteristic effect of the deliberate kind — belief in a false causal model — without a propagandist. This is also why the failed-consultation prediction of §13.5 must be stated carefully: the damage need not surface on procedural-fairness measures at all, only later in efficacy and in the accuracy of the citizen's causal model (§13.8).

Understanding therefore requires independent truth and ethical routing constraints.

## 12.5 No-compromise cases

Some joint constraint sets have no acceptable feasible region:

$$
\boxed{
\text{NO PERMISSIBLE COMPROMISE UNDER THE DECLARED CONSTRAINTS}
}
$$

is a legitimate result.

The framework is not a compromise fetish. It is a method for determining whether a real compromise exists after the representations stop hiding their costs.

## 12.6 The validator problem

The framework's load rests on adjudicating which variables are valid and which obligations are foreseeable — the five gates of §8.2 and the closure operator of §8.1. Yet the paper also insists that no viewpoint occupies the total view (§8; the correlated compression loss of §10.2). The method therefore cannot claim the neutral referee's seat it has just shown to be empty. Its honest status is narrower: constraint closure is a *discipline for making omissions visible and auditable*, not an oracle that certifies a uniquely correct constraint set. Every application of the gates is boundary-relative, contestable, and improvable; a *validated* position is one whose validator, panel, and residual disagreements are declared and open to challenge, not one that has escaped standpoint. A framework that concealed this would be one more coalition's cohesive total viewpoint claiming to be the neutral map — the exact failure it warns against. Naming the limit is what keeps it from becoming that.

# 13. An empirical program

The paper is currently a conceptual architecture. It earns stronger status only through measurement. This section first states the constructs operationally, then gives a falsifiable study for each load-bearing claim. Every prediction is **UNVERIFIED**; each is written with a pass, a fail, and an ambiguous outcome, so the program can lose.

## 13.1 Operationalizing the constructs

The theory is testable only if its objects become measurable. The proposed operationalizations:

| Construct | Symbol | Candidate measurement |
|---|---|---|
| Vibe | $\widehat M_{ic}$ | free-response and forced-choice reconstruction of the governing world-model a voter attributes to a candidate; low-dimensional embedding of the attributed traits |
| Resonance | $R_i(c)$ | rated experienced fit between $\widehat M_{ic}$ and the voter's own world, elicited separately from agreement and from predicted vote |
| Epistemic correspondence | $\phi$ | accuracy of the attributed model against the candidate's record and against realized outcomes |
| Semantic coverage | $\mathrm{Cov}_\Omega(A,B)$ | shared reconstructable concepts over a target domain $\Omega$, measured by cued reconstruction |
| Interoperability | — | accuracy with which A recovers B's intended claim after a bounded exchange |
| Constraint closure | $\mathrm{Cl}_\Omega(W)$ | share of a viewpoint's own foreseeable downstream obligations it spontaneously tracks within a declared boundary |
| Opposition checksum | $\mathrm{OC}$ | whether a discussant names, and later retains, the strongest valid variable the rival tracks |
| Perceived efficacy | $E$ | standard internal/external political-efficacy scales, measured longitudinally |
| Civic capacity | — | cross-group interoperability $\times$ retained corrective participation, held distinct from expressive intensity |

Three quantities must be kept apart throughout: **feeling heard**, **being causally influential**, and **being factually correct**. Collapsing them is the characteristic measurement error this program exists to avoid.

Two of these measures need a declared adjudicator, not an oracle. The closure denominator — a viewpoint's *complete* foreseeable-obligation set — and the valid/invalid classification of variables cannot be read off the neutral vantage the paper denies exists (§8; §12.6). In practice both are fixed by a pre-registered, boundary-relative expert-panel obligation list, and inter-coder reliability on $\mathrm{Valid}(x)$ and the hostile-control gate is itself a reported quantity. A closure or checksum score is therefore always relative to a declared panel and its measured agreement — never a claim to the total view.

## 13.2 Semantic reconstruction

Give participant A a structured political viewpoint and ask A to communicate it to B, manipulating shared cultural references and conceptual coverage. Measure separately: message length, reconstruction accuracy, repair turns, confidence, agreement, factual accuracy, and emotional recognition.

Prediction:

$$
\mathrm{Cov}_{\Omega}(A,B)\uparrow
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\mathbb E[\text{translation cost}]\downarrow
$$

for target objects inside the covered region.

- **Pass:** higher coverage lowers translation cost (fewer repair turns, higher reconstruction accuracy) independent of prior agreement.
- **Fail:** coverage has no effect, or its apparent effect is fully explained by prior agreement.
- **Ambiguous:** coverage helps only within-group, suggesting shared identity rather than shared concepts is doing the work.

## 13.3 Token divergence maps

Collect free-response expansions for contested terms across groups and compare relational structures, not word frequencies: what agents appear, what causal links are assumed, who is harmed, what history is loaded, what moral principle is activated, what identity is threatened.

- **Pass:** the same token reliably decodes to structurally different relational objects across groups, and that divergence predicts cross-group reconstruction failure.
- **Fail:** token expansions are structurally similar across groups, or their differences do not predict miscommunication.

## 13.4 Opposition-checksum intervention

Randomly assign political discussants to ordinary dialogue or a protocol requiring each to state (1) the strongest valid variable their own side tracks, (2) the strongest valid variable the other side tracks, (3) one downstream obligation their own principle generates, and (4) one observation that would narrow their preferred policy. Measure opponent-model accuracy, durable incorporation of the rival variable, discovery of third options, affective polarization, policy movement, factual learning, and willingness to impose costs on the out-group.

- **Pass:** the protocol raises durable rival-variable incorporation and third-option discovery relative to control, without a false-balance cost — participants do not adopt invalid variables at equal rates.
- **Fail:** the protocol shifts stated attitudes but not durable incorporation, or improves warmth only by inducing false balance.
- **Ambiguous:** effects appear immediately but decay to baseline, indicating a demand effect rather than model revision.

A full pre-registration of this study — hypotheses, power analysis, the validity-adjudication panel, and decision rules fixed before data collection — is developed in the companion note [*The opposition-checksum dialogue intervention: a pre-registration*](../research/opposition_checksum_intervention_preregistration_2026-08-09.md).

## 13.5 The civic participation–disillusionment trap

The §6 dynamics generate their own falsifiable predictions, each of which can fail.

- **Receipt effect.** Where institutions provide credible, specific evidence that citizen input affected a decision, efficacy and future participation should decay less after losses than under generic acknowledgment. *Fail:* credible receipts have no effect. *Ambiguous:* receipts matter only when the citizen's side wins, i.e. they act as consolation, not acknowledgment.
- **Causal-legibility effect.** Participation should persist more where the path from contribution to outcome is short and inspectable (some local or organizational settings) than where it is national, delayed, and diffuse.
- **Betrayal-to-sorting pathway.** Repeated perceived betrayal or nonresponse should predict migration toward more homogeneous political communities, especially where those communities supply immediate recognition and a coherent source of blame — the §6.3 mechanism.
- **Identity-fusion penalty.** Stronger self–party fusion should predict lower willingness to impose costs on one's own leaders for equivalent failures.
- **Conditional-loyalty advantage.** Groups that preserve long-horizon participation *and* maintain explicit internal replacement and criticism mechanisms should out-correct groups characterized by either automatic loyalty or rapid exit.
- **Failed-consultation backfire.** Visibly noncausal participation should reduce efficacy more than no invitation to participate at all.
- **Cross-channel resilience.** Citizens with several independent civic channels should be less likely to infer universal futility after failure in one.
- **Culture-war substitution.** As broad institutional participation loses causal legibility, expressive cultural participation may rise even when governing capacity does not — a direct test of *political intensity $\neq$ civic capacity*.

A serious test would separate outcome satisfaction, perceived procedural fairness, actual causal influence, perceived causal influence, identity recognition, factual accuracy, future participation, party loyalty, and cross-group interoperability, and would preregister pass, fail, and ambiguous outcomes rather than reporting whichever emerges.

## 13.6 Historical semantic-basis tomography

Use party platforms, speeches, newspapers, demographic data, election returns, religion, occupation, region, and education to estimate changing basis weights:

$$
B_j(t)=\sum_k w_{kj}(t)b_k(t).
$$

The target is not a single party-switch date but a map of which racial, religious, economic, regional, and status-coded vectors moved when. Goldwater's 1964 and 1981 records offer a useful within-person comparison of semantic drift under a stable label.

## 13.7 What would falsify the framework

The framework is not idle only if it forbids observable outcomes. It predicts, and can be sunk by, the following:

- coverage that does not lower translation cost (§13.2);
- contested tokens that decode identically across groups (§13.3);
- a checksum protocol that moves warmth but never durable model revision (§13.4);
- credible receipts that do not slow efficacy decay (§13.5);
- expressive intensity that tracks, rather than diverges from, governing capacity (§13.5).

Each would falsify a load-bearing claim rather than merely trim a parameter. The third is not hypothetical: warmth that never reaches durable behavior is the *modal* outcome of the affective-polarization intervention literature (§13.8), so the checksum protocol carries a heavier burden of proof than a neutral prior would assign.

## 13.8 Existing evidence and where it cuts

A literature-mining pass located adjacent empirical work bearing on the predictions above. The pattern is uniform enough to state plainly: **the "a divergence or mechanism exists" half of each prediction tends to be supported; the specific causal claim the paper actually stakes tends to be untested; and in two places the nearest large literature cuts against a naive reading.** Reporting only the supporting half would be the exact false-balance failure §12.1 forbids, so this subsection carries the counter-evidence too — the paper's own opposition-checksum discipline (§8.2) applied to itself.

**Efficacy erodes through experience (the §6 engine).** This has direct precedent. Soss (1999) shows that the *experience* of a demeaning, illegible bureaucratic process lowers political efficacy and participation relative to a rule-based one, through learned beliefs about government responsiveness rather than any information deficit. That corroborates the mechanism — efficacy is experience-sensitive, not merely ignorance-driven — without testing §6's specific loop, whose failing arm is failed *active* participation rather than passive administrative encounter. *Corroborated mechanism; the specific loop UNVERIFIED.*

**Receipts and causal legibility (§13.5).** Government responsiveness predicts re-participation: a successful first report on FixMyStreet raised the probability of a second by 54% (Sjöberg, Mellon & Peixoto 2015; n ≈ 399,000), and New York City participatory budgeting raised later voting by 8.4 points, most among usually-low-turnout groups (Johnson, Carlson & Reynolds 2023). Both are adjacent, not direct: they test receipt-versus-nothing inside single legible-local settings, whereas the paper stakes the finer receipt-versus-generic-acknowledgment contrast and an explicit local-versus-national comparison — neither of which any located study runs. *Corroborated by convergent adjacent findings; the stated contrasts UNVERIFIED.*

**The voice effect: a counter-finding the framework absorbs (§13.5).** The failed-consultation-backfire prediction meets a large, well-replicated literature that appears to cut against it. The procedural-justice *voice effect* (Lind & Tyler 1988) finds that being given voice raises perceived fairness of a process *even when the voice had no instrumental effect on the outcome*, often outweighing outcome favorability. Naively this contradicts "visibly noncausal participation reduces efficacy." It does not — it is this paper's §13.1 triad in the wild: *feeling heard* and *being causally influential* are separate quantities, and the voice effect is precisely a mechanism that manufactures the first while decoupling it from the second. That decoupling is emergent, accidental propaganda (§12.4): a legitimacy signal produced with no propagandist. The correct consequence is not to drop the prediction but to sharpen it — visibly noncausal consultation should *not* backfire on procedural-fairness measures (the voice effect buffers those) and may backfire only later, in efficacy and in the accuracy of the citizen's causal model. *UNVERIFIED, and the prediction is revised to separate procedural satisfaction from efficacy erosion; the voice-effect literature is construct-confirming, not refuting.*

**The checksum intervention's unfavorable base rate (§13.4).** No prior study tests the four-move checksum protocol; its form is novel, as the paper claims. But its family has a discouraging record: across large studies, including Voelkel et al. (2023; n = 8,385) and its associated megastudy, interventions that reduce affective warmth between partisans routinely fail to move durable, behaviorally consequential outcomes, and effects often decay within weeks. Durable dialogue effects *are* achievable — Broockman & Kalla (2016) durably shifted attitudes for months via narrative exchange — so the bar is not impossible; but "warmth without durable behavior" is the modal outcome, and durable *rival-variable incorporation* is a higher bar than warmth. *UNVERIFIED, and the nearest base rate is unfavorable: the §13.7 falsifier is the family's typical result, not a remote possibility.*

**Coverage lowers translation cost (§13.2).** The general mechanism is well established in psycholinguistics: accumulating common ground reduces referential effort and description length (Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs 1986). None of this is in the contested-political, adversarial domain, and none tests the "independent of prior agreement" clause. *Mechanism supported; the political-domain prediction UNVERIFIED.*

**Token divergence exists; that it predicts failure is untested (§13.3).** The first half is well supported, with examples close to the paper's own: partisans decode "systemic racism" as institutional causation versus personal accusation — a 42-point gap on whether racism requires institutional change (Pew Research Center 2021) — and same-ideology partisans show more similar neural segmentation of political words than cross-ideology pairs (de Bruin et al. 2023), consistent with differential moral-foundation weighting (Graham, Haidt & Nosek 2009). But the load-bearing, falsifiable half — that this divergence *predicts cross-group reconstruction failure* (§13.7) — has no located test. *Divergence SUPPORTED; the predictive-failure link UNVERIFIED.*

**Fusion, sorting, and conditional loyalty (§13.5) — the least-grounded.** Identity fusion predicts pro-group loyalty and tolerance of in-group wrongdoing (Swann et al. 2012), and partisans forgive co-partisan scandals more than equivalent out-party ones — adjacent to the identity-fusion penalty, but no located study regresses a *fusion measure* directly on *willingness to hold one's own leaders accountable*. The betrayal-to-sorting pathway has qualitative support (Cramer 2016) but a real quantitative complication: stated preferences to live among co-partisans diverge sharply from actual moving behavior, which is weak and concentrated among the most extreme (Mummolo & Nall 2017), so "betrayal drives sorting" must not be read past what behavior supports. The conditional-loyalty advantage — a bespoke comparative-institutions claim — has no located direct test at all, and is the prediction most in need of a purpose-built study. *Identity-fusion penalty PARTIALLY SUPPORTED but untested as stated; betrayal-to-sorting MIXED, with the preference–behavior gap flagged; conditional-loyalty advantage UNVERIFIED.*

The upshot is not that the framework is confirmed. Its *descriptive* premises — divergent decoding, experience-driven efficacy loss, responsiveness-sensitive participation — stand on real ground, while its *interventional and predictive* claims remain genuinely open, and in two cases face an unfavorable prior the paper is better served by naming than by omitting.

# 14. Conclusion

Politics emerges from a population that does not share one internal language.

Citizens occupy the same reality through different locations, histories, toolkits, identities, and compressions. Candidates win partly by emitting representations coherent with enough of those private worlds. Parties carry coalitions whose contents can change while their names remain. Culture war intensifies when each coalition becomes fluent inside its own semantic library and increasingly unable—or unwilling—to reconstruct the other's.

This does not mean every political conflict is a misunderstanding. Translation can expose a sharper empirical disagreement, a genuine moral collision, a material interest, a rights boundary, or a strategic attempt at domination.

But many public positions remain artificially coherent by throwing away the strongest variable tracked by their rivals and by terminating their own principles at a convenient causal boundary.

Constraint-closed compromise demands more.

Each side must:

- reconstruct the other's object before judging it;
- accept the foreseeable obligations generated by its own principle;
- incorporate the strongest rival variable that survives validation;
- state where facts, weights, rights, and interests still conflict;
- allow the process to return no compromise.

This is not moderation for its own sake. It is representational honesty.

A democracy does not require everyone to think the same thing. It requires enough semantic infrastructure that citizens can make their worlds mutually visible, enough truth discipline to reject shared falsehood, and enough moral closure that no coalition may preserve its purity by discarding the people and consequences its own principles create.

The purpose of a cohesive total viewpoint is not to end disagreement.

It is to make disagreement answerable to a larger share of reality.
